# Let Claude Plan, Let Code Pull the Trigger

Day trading on 1-minute and 5-minute charts is about the moment, but the "moment" comes in two speeds, and the evidence treats them very differently. At the **seconds scale**, which covers tape reading, Level 2 and 10-second "micro pullback" scalps, professional high-frequency firms set the price. The predictive window of order-flow signals shrank from **about 30 minutes in 1996 to about 5 minutes by 2002**, and it has almost certainly shrunk further since. At the **minutes-to-hours scale**, a few rule-based setups do have published backtests: the **5-minute opening range breakout on "stocks in play"**, **trading with VWAP** on index funds, and **"first half-hour predicts last half-hour"** intraday momentum. The ORB and VWAP backtests come from authors who also sell trading education, and one independent replication of ORB on stock indices found **gross profits but roughly zero net of costs**. Costs are the central problem. Measured retail round trips cost **7 to 46 basis points (0.07–0.46%) depending only on the broker**. On a trade aiming for a 0.2% move, that can push the win rate needed to break even to 75%. The real-world outcome studies are bleak: **97% of persistent day traders in Brazil lost money**, and **fewer than 1%** of Taiwanese day traders were reliably profitable. For Trader HQ, the right design is not a faster Claude. Claude should act as a **planner** that, every few minutes, picks which stocks are in play and writes exact trigger, stop, target and expiry levels. A small piece of deterministic code, the **trigger engine**, watches Alpaca's live stream and fires a protected bracket order the instant a level breaks. Today's bot looks at 5-minute data every 15 minutes, so it can act on a signal that is already up to 15 minutes old, and it computes VWAP from IEX data that covers about 2% of market volume. Both are fixable. A paid SIP data plan matters more than any prompt tweak, and the whole experiment should stay small, paper-first and measured against simply holding SPY.

**How to read this report.** Claims are labeled **(evidence)** for peer-reviewed studies, regulator data or rule text; **(vendor-backed study)** for backtests written by people who sell trading courses; **(practitioner claim)** for rules taught by trading educators without independent testing; and **(inference)** for reasoning or arithmetic. The earlier Trader HQ reports covered candle basics, order types, the end of the pattern-day-trader rule and the case for an index-fund core. This one builds on those and focuses only on what changes when the chart is 1 or 5 minutes wide.

## A live screen answers four questions, in order

Watching a fast chart feels like reading a story as it is written. Experienced momentum traders impose an order on it, and the order matters more than any single indicator. A typical screen has five parts: a **scanner** that finds stocks "in play", a **1-minute and 5-minute candle chart** with VWAP and the 9- and 20-period EMAs, a set of **horizontal levels**, a **Level 2** window and a **time & sales** window (practitioner claim; [Warrior Trading](https://www.warriortrading.com/bull-flag-trading/); [daytradingtoolkit](https://daytradingtoolkit.com/beginners-guide/level-2-quotes-time-sales-order-book)).

A few terms, in plain English. A **1-minute candle** is one candle per minute of trading. A **tick chart** draws a new candle every N trades instead of every N seconds, so it speeds up when the stock gets busy and slows down when it goes quiet. **VWAP** (volume-weighted average price) is the average price paid for every share traded today, weighting each price by how many shares changed hands there. Traders treat it as the day's "fair price": buyers are in control above it, sellers below. **EMAs** (exponential moving averages) are averages of recent closes that weight the newest candles most. On a 1-minute chart, the 9 EMA is roughly the last 9 minutes. **Relative volume (RVOL)** is today's volume divided by the normal volume for this time of day. An RVOL of 5 means five times busier than usual. **Level 2** shows the resting buy and sell orders waiting at each price, and **time & sales** (the "tape") lists every trade as it prints, with price, size and time. Brokers color each print by whether it hit the ask (buyers paying up, green) or the bid (sellers dumping, red) (evidence: broker documentation; [Schwab StreetSmart Pro](https://help.streetsmart.schwab.com/pro/4.38/Content/Level_II_Quotes_and_Time_&_Sales.htm)).

The useful reading order for a beginner is a set of four questions. **First, is this stock in play?** That means unusual relative volume plus a reason, usually news. Without that, 1-minute candles are mostly noise. **Second, where are the levels?** Mark the premarket high and low, yesterday's close, VWAP and the high of day (HOD). Fast setups almost always trigger at one of these. **Third, what are the 1-minute and 5-minute candles doing at that level?** Is price pushing through it on rising volume, or stalling with long upper wicks? **Fourth, does the tape agree?** If prints are hitting the ask and a big bid on Level 2 is actually being traded against, buyers are real. If a large bid sits on Level 2 but no prints ever hit it, educators warn it may be passive or fake ("spoofing") (practitioner claim; [daytradingtoolkit](https://daytradingtoolkit.com/beginners-guide/level-2-quotes-time-sales-order-book)). This is inference built on the practitioner material above, but it matches how both systematic and discretionary sources define the job.

The research on the fourth question is the most sobering. Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov showed with NYSE data that, over short intervals, price changes are driven mainly by **order-flow imbalance**: the gap between buying and selling pressure at the best bid and ask. The link is linear and stable across stocks (evidence; [Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)). That sounds like a vindication of tape reading. The catch is that the imbalance *explains* the price move during the same interval. It does not forecast the next one. Chordia, Roll and Subrahmanyam measured how long lagged imbalances kept predicting returns: **up to 30 minutes in 1996, 10 minutes in 1999, and only 5 minutes in 2002**, as faster traders learned to trade the imbalance away (evidence; [Chordia, Roll & Subrahmanyam, JFE 2005](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8wb6140g/qt8wb6140g.pdf?t=krndan)). No later replication turned up, but after two decades of colocated high-frequency trading, any edge from watching Level 2 by eye is most likely measured in seconds (inference). Firm-level data on high-frequency traders shows why. **Relative speed explains large differences in their profits**, and firms that paid for faster colocation improved their results (evidence, Swedish data; [Baron, Brogaard, Hagströmer & Kirilenko, JFQA 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v54y2019i03p993-1024_00.html)).

So reading the tape is a genuine skill, but its useful job for a retail trader is *confirmation*: avoid a breakout no one is buying. It is not a source of profit on its own. For a bot, it is the part least worth automating.

## Only three fast setups have published rules and backtests

Trading educators teach a dozen named setups with precise-sounding rules. Most have never been independently tested. The table sorts them by the strength of the evidence, and the gap between the first three rows and the rest is the most important thing in this report.

| Setup | Chart and entry | Stop | Exit / hold time | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **5-min opening range breakout (ORB) on stocks in play** | If the first 5-minute candle is green, buy as price breaks its high; if red, short below its low | 10% of the 14-day ATR from entry | At the close if not stopped: minutes to about 6.5 hours | Vendor-backed study, 7,000+ US stocks, 2016–2023 ([Zarattini, Barbon & Aziz](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:chf:rpseri:rp2498); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=45379)) |
| **VWAP trend** | Long while price is above VWAP, flat or short below | Opposite VWAP cross | Until the VWAP cross: minutes to hours | Vendor-backed study on QQQ only ([Concretum Group](https://concretumgroup.com/volume-weighted-average-price-vwap-the-holy-grail-for-day-trading-systems/)) |
| **Intraday momentum** | If SPY is up from yesterday's close to 10:00, hold SPY for the last half-hour | None in the study | About 30 minutes | Evidence, peer-reviewed ([Gao, Han, Li & Zhou, JFE 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:129:y:2018:i:2:p:394-414)) |
| Bull flag / micro pullback | After 2–3 red candles, buy the first 1-min (or 10-sec) candle that makes a new high | Low of the pullback | Retest of high of day: seconds to minutes | Practitioner claim ([Warrior Trading](https://www.warriortrading.com/bull-flag-trading/)) |
| VWAP reversal | 30–45 min after open, buy a higher high after price reclaims VWAP on volume | Just under the prior candle | First target 2R, partials at 5-min averages | Practitioner claim ([Bear Bull Traders](https://bearbulltraders.com/?p=19639)) |
| ABCD | Spike (A), 50–70% pullback (B), higher low (C); buy the break of B | Below C | "Up to your style" | Practitioner claim ([Bear Bull Traders PDF](https://bearbulltraders.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/ABCD-pattern.pdf)) |
| Gap-and-go | Stock gaps 10%+ on news; buy above the premarket high | Under the premarket pullback | Minutes | Practitioner claim ([TradingView summary](https://de.tradingview.com/script/mbqMf3pF-Ross-Cameron-5-Pillars-Filter)) |
| Red-to-green | Stock opens below yesterday's close and reclaims it | Not specified | Not specified | Practitioner claim ([Bear Bull Traders slides](https://bearbulltraders.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Session-4.pdf)) |

(R is the distance from entry to stop. A 2R target is twice that distance. ATR, average true range, is how far the stock typically moves in a day.)

### The ORB result is the best evidence on individual stocks, and it is fragile

The opening range breakout paper is the strongest case for fast trading in single stocks. Restricting the trade to stocks in play, meaning the 20 names with the highest opening relative volume among stocks above **$5**, with **1M+ shares** of average daily volume and an ATR above **$0.50**, turned a mediocre strategy into a reported **1,600%+ net return with a Sharpe ratio of 2.81** over 2016–2023 (vendor-backed study; [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/individual-investing/intraday-trading-of-overactive-stocks-via-opening-range-breakout/); [University of St. Gallen PDF](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/3c2989c4-688d-4d78-8a71-f02690990d51/content)). A Sharpe ratio measures return per unit of volatility, and anything above 2 is extraordinary. That alone should make a reader skeptical. Two of the three authors run trading-education businesses. The exact cost assumptions were not verified. And a third-party replication on five stock *indices* reproduced the gross profits but found **net returns of about zero after costs**, although it tested indices, not stocks in play ([mql5 blog title](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/776235)). The VWAP-trend paper reported that **$25,000 became $192,656 on QQQ** over 2018–2023, but a public reproduction assumed **zero slippage** ([Concretum Group](https://concretumgroup.com/volume-weighted-average-price-vwap-the-holy-grail-for-day-trading-systems/); [QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/terminal/cache/embedded_backtest_b115d0894231d55994b1068202f6c0ae.html)).

The intraday momentum study is the most academically solid and also the most modest. The first half-hour explains only **about 1.6% of the variation** in the last half-hour's return. The trading rule earned **6.3% a year before costs** versus −0.5% for always holding the last half-hour, and it worked better on volatile, high-volume, news-heavy days (evidence; [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/2014/08/attention-prop-traders-the-first-half-hour-of-trading-predicts-the-last-half-hour/)).

Look at what the credible results have in common. They **enter fast but exit slowly**: they hold for 30 minutes or until the close, not for seconds. They trade liquid instruments. And they follow mechanical rules that a computer can check. The seconds-long scalps that dominate beginner YouTube, such as micro pullbacks on 10-second charts, have rules but no independent evidence behind them (inference from the notes' search; [Warrior Trading](https://www.warriortrading.com/bull-flag-trading/)).

### Small-cap "in play" stocks carry risks a stop cannot handle

The academic and retail versions of "stocks in play" differ in a way that matters. The ORB study required a price above $5 and heavy normal volume. The popular retail checklist hunts for stocks priced **$1–$20**, with a **float under 10 million shares** (float is the number of shares available to trade), **5x relative volume** and **up 10% or more** on news (practitioner claim; [TradingView "5 Pillars"](https://de.tradingview.com/script/mbqMf3pF-Ross-Cameron-5-Pillars-Filter)). The positive backtests therefore do not carry over to the cheap low-float runners that educators promote (inference).

That universe brings three hazards. The first is **halts**. Under the Limit Up-Limit Down (LULD) rules, a non-S&P stock above $3 has a **10% price band** (20% from $0.75 to $3), the bands **double in the opening 15 and closing 25 minutes**, and a stock stuck at its band for 15 seconds is **paused for 5 minutes** (rule; [Nasdaq LULD FAQ](https://m.nasdaqtrader.com/content/MarketRegulation/LULD_FAQ.pdf); [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/measures)). Sources disagree on exactly when bands double, but the risk is the same either way: a halted stock can reopen far through your stop. The second is **dilution**. Small companies often sell new shares *into* a price spike through offerings or at-the-market programs, which caps the rally (practitioner claim; [Guy Gentile guide](https://guygentile.com/guides/stock-dilution-and-offerings)). The third is **ramp-and-dump schemes**: FINRA flagged unusual price spikes around small-cap IPOs where foreign brokers held most of the shares and sold at the peak (rule/regulator; [FINRA Regulatory Notice 22-25](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/2022-11/Regulatory-Notice-22-25.pdf)). Shorting a collapsing stock has its own trap. Once a stock is down 10% from yesterday's close, **Rule 201** allows short sales only above the best bid for the rest of that day and the next (rule; [SEC press release](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2010/2010-26.htm)).

## Costs, not chart skill, decide most fast trades

The cost of one round trip (a buy plus a sell) has three parts: the **spread** (the gap between the best bid and the best ask, which you pay by crossing it), **slippage** (getting filled worse than the price you saw) and **fees**. In 2026 the fees are trivial. The SEC's Section 31 fee is **$20.60 per $1 million sold** from April 4, 2026, and FINRA's trading activity fee is **$0.000195 per share sold, capped at $9.79** (rule; [SEC order 34-104909](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/34-104909.pdf); [Revolut help](https://help.revolut.com/en-BG/help/wealth/order-execution-fees-and-limits/trading-regulatory-fees/)). FINRA also proposed pausing that fee for October–December 2026, but its status was not confirmed ([SR-FINRA-2026-021](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/2026-09/SR-FINRA-2026-021.pdf)). Selling $10,000 of stock costs about 21 cents in fees (inference).

The spread is what hurts. The best audited evidence comes from Schwarz, Barber, Huang, Jorion and Odean, who placed **85,000 identical market orders** at five brokers simultaneously. The average round-trip cost ranged from **0.07% to 0.46%**, before commissions, and **depended on the broker**, because wholesalers gave different brokers different prices for the same trade. Payment for order flow did not explain the gap (evidence; [Journal of Finance 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v80y2025i5p2507-2541.html); [WashU Olin](https://olin.washu.edu/about/news-and-media/news/2022/10/study-finds-astonishing-differences-in-brokers-pricing-of-stock-trades.php)). Spread size also depends heavily on the stock. Many liquid stocks above $1 quote an average spread of **$0.015 or less** (rule; [SEC fact sheet](https://sec.gov/files/34-101070-fact-sheet.pdf)), which is about 2 basis points on a $50 stock. A $0.05 spread on a $5 small cap is 100 basis points, and a $0.10 spread on a $2 runner is 500 (inference). The half-penny tick that would narrow spreads in liquid names has been **pushed back to November 2027** and is under review, so a bot should not count on it ([Federal Register 2026-11997](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-11997.pdf)).

### The break-even win rate climbs fast as targets shrink

The arithmetic is simple and unforgiving. If T is the profit target, S is the stop distance and c is the all-in round-trip cost, the share of trades you must win just to break even is **(S + c) ÷ (T + S)** (inference).

| Target | Stop | Round-trip cost | Must win to break even | Typical case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.20% | 0.20% | 0.04% | 60% | Liquid large cap, 1-cent spread |
| 0.20% | 0.20% | 0.10% | 75% | Same scalp at a worse broker |
| 0.50% | 0.50% | 0.10% | 60% | 5-minute trade, liquid stock |
| 2.00% | 1.00% | 0.10% | 37% | ORB-style "let it run" trade |
| 2.00% | 2.00% | 1.00% | 75% | $5 small cap with a 5-cent spread |

This table makes the core argument of the report. A 1-to-5-minute scalp aiming for 0.2% in a liquid stock needs to win far more than half the time just to stay even, so the trader is betting on being right *and* fast against firms that are faster. A trade that aims for several times its stop, held for longer, can tolerate costs. That is exactly the shape of the setups with published evidence. Costs also scale with frequency: twenty round trips a day at 0.1% each burns **2% of the money traded every day** (inference).

Stops cost more than targets. The SEC warns that a stop price "is not the guaranteed execution price": it triggers a market order, and the fill "can deviate significantly from the stop price in a fast-moving market." A stop-limit order caps the price but may never fill (rule/regulator education; [SEC investor bulletin](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20121014015731/http://www.sec.gov/investor/alerts/trading101basics.pdf)). Stops fire precisely when liquidity is thin and the price is moving against you. A sensible backtest charges at least the full spread plus one or two extra cents of slippage on every stop exit (inference).

### The outcome studies say most people lose, and LLMs are no shortcut

The best data on real traders is consistent. In Brazil, among people who day-traded index futures for more than 300 days, **97% lost money** and only **1.1% earned more than the minimum wage**. The study found no sign that traders improved with experience (evidence; [Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101)). In Taiwan, **fewer than 1%** of day traders earned predictably positive returns after fees, and day traders as a group lost money in every one of 15 years (evidence; [Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:1-24)). In the US, stocks that Robinhood users piled into on a given day went on to underperform. In one version of the study, top buys lost about **4.7% over the next 20 days** relative to the market (evidence; [Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/SSRN-id3715077.pdf)). That matters for any bot that buys "top movers": hold a scalp past the session and you drift toward the documented reversal (inference). No study isolates traders who hold for only seconds or minutes, so the exact odds for scalpers are unknown.

AI traders have not escaped the base rate. In the public Alpha Arena contest, **four of six** frontier models lost money trading crypto, and in a later US-stock season, reports say only **6 of 32 results** were positive. One model made **1,418 trades** in a round versus 158 for the winner, an example of a model overtrading when left to choose how often to trade (news reports; [Forklog](https://forklog.com/en/four-out-of-six-ai-models-suffer-losses-in-trading-tournament/); [KuCoin news](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/ai-trading-models-struggle-in-live-market-tests-majority-of-systems-report-losses); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/markets/news/ai-bots-auditioning-for-wall-street-trading-are-mostly-losing-money-126050701793_1.html)). The organizers themselves said the contests were too short to rank models. Academic benchmarks find some LLM agents beat buy-and-hold, but **not consistently** (evidence; [StockBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02209v1)), and models that score well on static tests did *worse* live (evidence; [LiveTradeBench](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2511.03628)). None of these tested 1-minute or 5-minute trading. Backtests that let a language model "replay" past days are also contaminated, because the model may remember what happened (evidence; [Look-Ahead-Bench review](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/look-ahead-bench-a-standardized-benchmark-of-look-ahead-bias-in-point-in-time-llms-for-finance)).

One rule change cuts the other way. The SEC approved the end of the **$25,000 pattern-day-trader minimum** on April 14, 2026, effective **June 4, 2026**, and Alpaca switched to an intraday margin framework that day (rule/broker; [Alpaca blog](https://alpaca.markets/blog/finra-retires-the-pdt-rule-introducing-alpacas-new-intraday-margin-framework/); [ThinkAdvisor](https://www.thinkadvisor.com/amp/2026/04/14/sec-approves-plan-removing-day-trading-limit-for-small-investors/)). A small account can now day-trade without limit. Given the outcome data, that mostly means the bot's own trade caps are now the only brake (inference).

## Claude plans every few minutes; a trigger engine acts in milliseconds

The current Trader HQ day bot asks Claude to look at 5-minute bars every 15 minutes and, if it likes a setup, sends a limit bracket buy. Three problems follow. **Timing**: a breakout that happens at 10:01 is not seen until about 10:15, by which point the entry has either run away or failed. **Data**: the bot requests the free IEX feed, and IEX carries about **2% of US volume**, so VWAP and "last bar volume vs average" are computed from a small, noisy sample, and some minutes have no IEX bar at all (Alpaca staff; [Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/how-accurate-is-get-stock-latest-bar/14122)). **Role**: Claude is doing the timing job, which is the job it is worst suited to. An LLM call takes seconds, gives different answers to the same input, and has no demonstrated edge at intraday timescales (inference from the benchmarks above). Its plausible value is selection and discipline: which names are in play, what kind of day it is, and when to stand aside.

### Split the bot into three parts with one live data feed

The design below is engineering inference built on Alpaca's documented capabilities. No published Alpaca reference architecture for LLM-plus-executor bots was found.

The **planner (Claude)** runs every 5 minutes during the trading window and also on events such as a halt resuming, a fill or a stop-out. It receives a compact summary: opening range, VWAP, high of day, premarket levels, RVOL, spread, ATR, news flags, open positions, today's P&L and remaining risk budget. It returns strict JSON *plans*, not orders: `{symbol, setup, side, trigger_price, entry_limit, stop, target, max_risk_usd, time_stop_min, valid_until, invalidate_if}`. Code validates every plan and **rejects, never "fixes,"** a bad one: the stop must be on the correct side, risk must be within caps, the reward-to-risk ratio must meet the minimum, the symbol must be on the allowed list, the plan must not have expired, and the cost-to-target ratio must be acceptable.

The **trigger engine (code)** holds the live plans and listens to Alpaca's single market-data websocket. Alpaca allows only **one connection** per data endpoint on most plans, so one process must fan the data out to everything else (docs; [Alpaca WebSocket Stream](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/streaming-market-data)). Minute bars arrive **20–100 milliseconds** after each minute ends. The engine acts on that first bar and treats the `updatedBars` correction, which can follow about 30 seconds later, as a data fix rather than a new signal, so a trigger never fires twice (Alpaca staff; [Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/alpaca-market-data-update-bars/8947)). When a plan's condition is met and the spread and halt checks pass, it sends one order with a deterministic `client_order_id` (for example `2026-11-04-plan17-entry-1`). The engine never invents a trade. If Claude is slow or down, existing positions keep their protection and plans simply expire.

The **risk governor (code)** sits outside both and can override them. It enforces daily limits, reads Alpaca's `trade_updates` stream for fills (never polling, because the trading API allows about **200 calls per minute**), reconciles orders and positions after every reconnect, and owns the kill switch: cancel all orders and close all positions (Alpaca staff and docs; [Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/what-is-the-api-call-limit-for-free-tier-market-data/5708); [Alpaca websocket docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/websocket-streaming)). Alpaca's own guidance is to write each order intent locally *before* sending it, look orders up by `client_order_id` after a lost response instead of resending, and treat duplicate stream events as no-ops ([alpaca-skills reconciliation guide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/alpacahq/alpaca-skills/main/skills/broker-api/reconciliation-idempotency/SKILL.md)). A replace (edit) of an order has been reported to leave the original able to fill, creating a double position, so it is safer to cancel, confirm, then send a new order ([Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/replace-order-creates-a-new-order-successfully-but-the-old-order-is-filled-later-thus-creating-duplicate-order/5794)).

Every entry carries its exits on Alpaca's servers. A **bracket order** holds the entry, a take-profit limit and a stop-loss. An **OTO** order holds the entry plus only a stop, for setups where code manages the exit. Neither works in extended hours, so the fast bot should trade **regular hours only** (docs; [Alpaca orders](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/orders-at-alpaca)). Enter with a **marketable limit**: a limit a few cents or 0.1–0.2% above the ask, so a runaway price produces a missed trade instead of a terrible fill. Size every later step from the *filled* quantity, because partial fills are routine (inference).

| Target | No fixed target in the study: OTO stop plus a code time exit; optionally a far target at 3R | 2R take-profit | None |
| Time stop | Flatten at the bot's end-of-day time | Exit if not at +1R within 20–30 minutes (inference, to test) | Exit at about 15:55 |
| Orders | OTO (entry + stop), code-managed exit | Bracket | Marketable limit; exit at close |

Shorts stay off until the long side has proven itself, because of Rule 201 and borrow issues. The red-candle half of ORB is left out for the same reason. Setup C conflicts with the current settings (`no_new_entries_min_before_close: 30` and `flatten_min_before_close: 10`), so it needs its own exception or its own bot. It is worth running because it is a near-free test of whether the plumbing and cost tracking work on a rule with academic backing.

Do **not** automate micro-pullback scalps on 10-second charts, Level 2 or tape signals, low-float gappers under $5, or the opening 5 minutes before the range forms. Their evidence is the weakest, their costs are highest, and they depend on speed the system does not have.

### Filters every trigger must pass

Before any order, the engine checks five things. **Spread**: the current spread is no more than a set share of the planned target, so that estimated round-trip cost ÷ target stays at or below 0.25, and an absolute cap of about 0.05% in liquid names (inference). **Halt status**: the stock is trading normally, with no recent LULD pause and no pending halt, using Alpaca's `statuses` and `lulds` channels (docs; [Alpaca WebSocket Stream](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/streaming-market-data)). **Freshness**: the last data message is under about 5 seconds old, otherwise new entries are blocked. **Market regime**: for longs, SPY is above its own VWAP, so the bot is not buying breakouts into a falling market (practitioner logic). **Budget**: risk, position and trade-count limits all have room.

### Risk controls with numbers

Today's defaults include `daily_loss_limit_pct: 0`, which means the day bot's own daily loss limit is **off**. A fast bot needs these on from day one. The numbers below are starting points chosen by inference, not tested optima.

| Control | Suggested start |
|---|---|
| Risk per trade | 0.25–0.5% of the bot's allocated capital (keep the current $25 cap while learning) |
| Daily loss limit | 1.5% of allocated capital, then flatten and stop for the day |
| Max entries per day | 4–6 (current 6 is fine) |
| Max open positions | 2 |
| Per-symbol | Stop trading a symbol after one stop-out that day |
| Cooldown | 30 minutes after two stops in a row |
| Gap-risk sizing | For any Tier 2 or news-driven stock, size so that a move of 2× the stop distance through a halt is still tolerable |
| Kill switch | Manual button plus automatic trigger on stale data, repeated order rejects, or local positions not matching Alpaca's |
| Rate limit | Token bucket of at most about 60 order actions per minute, far under Alpaca's 200 |

### Buy SIP data before trusting VWAP or volume

The free Basic plan streams IEX only, for **up to 30 symbols on one connection**. Algo Trader Plus streams the full consolidated SIP feed for **unlimited symbols** and raises market-data API calls from about 200 to 10,000 per minute (docs and Alpaca staff; [Alpaca market data docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/about-market-data-api); [Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/what-is-the-api-call-limit-for-free-tier-market-data/5708)). It costs about **$99 a month** according to a third-party comparison ([PyQuant News](https://www.pyquantnews.com/free-python-resources/alpaca-pricing-plans-compared-python-data-examples-limits)). Both recommended stock setups depend on volume, through VWAP and relative volume, so **SIP is effectively required to trade them live** (inference). The free plan can still serve two purposes. It can run Setup C, which only needs SPY's price. And free historical SIP data older than 15 minutes is enough for backtests (docs, re-verify; [Alpaca market data docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/about-market-data-api)).

The data bill should be in the business case. $99 a month is **$1,188 a year**, a 24% annual hurdle on a $5,000 trading allocation before a single spread is paid (inference). That is the strongest argument for keeping the bot on paper with free data until the rules show an edge in backtests, and only then paying for SIP to run a live trial.

### Test in four stages, and expect each to flatter

Start with **backtests on 1-minute SIP bars**. Fill a signal from bar t only at bar t+1. When the stop and the target both fall inside one bar, assume the stop hit first. Charge the half-spread plus slippage on each side, with extra on stops and at the open. Never let Claude replay past dates (inference; [Alpaca forum on bar revisions](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/alpaca-alters-past-bars-trades-value/15456)). Next, **paper trade** to test plumbing: brackets, partial fills, reconnects and the kill switch. Do not treat paper P&L as real. Alpaca's paper engine fills at the quoted bid or ask with random partial fills and no market-impact model, and users have reported fill delays of **50–260 seconds** (docs and forum; [Alpaca paper trading](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading); [Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/paper-trading-fill-delays-of-50-260-seconds-limit-orders-filled-minutes-after-price-crossed/18223)). Then go **live at tiny size** to measure real slippage, logging each fill against the bid-ask midpoint at the moment the order was sent. Only then consider scaling up, and judge the bot against the SPY shadow account described in the earlier Trader HQ guide. Brokers' new Rule 605 reports, which from **August 2026** include realized spreads at 1-second to 5-minute horizons, are the right yardstick for whether Alpaca's fills are competitive ([Federal Register 2026-06569](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-04-06/pdf/2026-06569.pdf)).

## Conclusion

The intuition that day trading is "about the chart in the moment" is half right, and the wrong half is expensive. The moment that a human eye or a language model can act on is minutes long. The moment where tape reading pays is seconds long and belongs to firms that pay for colocation. The setups with any published support enter on a sharp, mechanical trigger and then *wait*: until the close, a VWAP cross, or the last half-hour. Fast decisions with slow exits is a shape a cheap, deterministic trigger engine can execute well, and it lets Claude do what it is actually good at, which is reading the context of the day and saying no.

The most useful change to Trader HQ is therefore architectural and statistical, not a cleverer prompt. Move timing out of Claude, turn the loss limits on, measure every fill's real cost, and treat the $99 data plan and the 75% break-even win rates as the hurdles they are. A fast bot that trades less, on fewer and more liquid names, with exits it cannot forget, has a chance of losing slowly enough to learn whether it has an edge. That is the honest goal for the first few hundred trades.

## Beginner checklist

**Reading a fast chart**

1. Ask "is it in play?" first: relative volume well above normal and a real news reason. If not, close the chart.
2. Mark the levels before the open: premarket high and low, yesterday's close, and later VWAP and the high of day.
3. Use the 5-minute chart for the story and the 1-minute chart for the trigger. Treat 10-second and tick charts as optional.
4. Read Level 2 and time & sales only as confirmation. Big bids that never trade mean nothing.
5. Skip stocks under $5 and low-float runners until you have months of logged results. Halts and offerings beat stops.

**Before any trade, human or bot**

1. Write the trigger, entry limit, stop, target, time stop and expiry before entering.
2. Compute cost ÷ target. If it is above 0.25, skip the trade.
3. Check the break-even win rate: (stop + cost) ÷ (target + stop).
4. Size from the stop: risk per trade ÷ (entry − stop), using the filled quantity.
5. Use marketable limit entries and broker-side brackets or OTO stops, regular hours only.

**For the Trader HQ day bot**

1. Separate the planner (Claude, every 5 minutes, outputs validated JSON plans) from the trigger engine (code, one websocket, fires instantly).
2. Start with long-only 5-minute ORB on liquid stocks in play and VWAP pullbacks on the watchlist. Add last-half-hour SPY as a control.
3. Set `daily_loss_limit_pct` above 0, cap open positions at 2, add a cooldown after two stops, and stop trading a symbol after one stop-out.
4. Add halt, spread and stale-data checks, deterministic `client_order_id`s, reconciliation on reconnect, and a kill switch.
5. Backtest on free historical SIP bars with next-bar fills and stop-first assumptions. Never backtest by having Claude replay past days.
6. Paper-trade to test the plumbing, not the profits. Then go live tiny and log each fill against the midpoint.
7. Pay for SIP data only once the backtest survives costs, and count the $99 a month in the bot's results.
8. Judge the bot after hundreds of trades against a SPY shadow account. If it cannot beat that after costs, turn it off.
