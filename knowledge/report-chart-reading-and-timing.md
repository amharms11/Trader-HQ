# Read Candles for Context, Not Prophecy

A candlestick chart is a compact record of what price did. It is not a forecast, and that sets the rules for everything else in this report. Each candle shows four prices for one time period: the open, the high, the low and the close. Learning to read them takes an afternoon. Rigorous tests of the famous candle patterns, though, mostly find **no reliable edge on US stocks**. Most named patterns perform close to a coin flip, and a few behave opposite to their textbook meaning. The tools with the strongest research behind them are not about prediction. Support and resistance levels, moving averages and volatility measures such as ATR are useful because they tell you **where you are wrong and how much to risk**. On timing, the evidence is clearer. The first 15–30 minutes after the 9:30 a.m. ET open are the most expensive and noisiest time to trade. The close is the deepest pool of liquidity. Historically most stock-index gains have come overnight, which favors swing trades over day trades. The base rate matters too: large studies find that **fewer than 1% of day traders are reliably profitable** after fees. So the most valuable thing a chart gives you is a set of written rules for entering, sizing and exiting, and your Claude-assisted Alpaca bots can enforce those rules better than willpower can. Treat paper-trading results as a best case until the rules survive real costs.

## Every candle compresses four prices into one shape

A candle has two parts. The **body** is the thick box between the open and the close. The **wicks**, also called shadows, are the thin lines above and below the body, and they mark the highest and lowest prices traded in that period. A green (or white/hollow) body means the stock closed *above* where it opened. A red (or black/filled) body means it closed *below* its open. A candle can have no wick at all, or a body squashed to a flat line when open and close are equal ([Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/learning-center/Idenitfying-Chart-Patterns.pdf); [Schwab](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/how-to-read-stock-charts-and-trading-patterns)). Bar charts show exactly the same open-high-low-close (OHLC) data in a different style, so choosing candles is about readability, not extra information ([Fidelity/Stocks & Commodities](https://www.fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/CandlesticksvsBarcharts.pdf)).

Beginners often misread color. On most platforms color compares a candle's close with **its own open**, not with yesterday's close. Suppose a stock gaps down at the open and then rallies all day but still finishes below yesterday's close. It prints a green candle on a day it lost money. Some apps offer "hollow" candles that color relative to the prior close instead, so check your app's setting. Also check whether your daily candles include pre-market and after-hours trading. Many platforms leave extended hours out by default, which is why a daily chart can show a dramatic "gap" between one day's close and the next day's open.

Timeframes are the same data at different zoom levels. The regular US session runs 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. ET, which is 390 minutes. So one daily candle contains 390 one-minute candles, 78 five-minute candles, or 26 fifteen-minute candles. A weekly candle opens at Monday's open, closes at Friday's close, and its wicks reach the week's extremes. Monthly candles work the same way. The practical consequence is that a "pattern" on one timeframe is just detail on another. Take a day that sold off hard in the morning and recovered by the close. It shows up on the daily chart as one candle with a long lower wick. On the 5-minute chart it is dozens of red candles followed by dozens of green ones. Timeframes can also disagree, for example a bullish 15-minute signal inside a bearish daily trend. The usual practitioner advice is to read the higher timeframe first for direction and then drop down to time the entry. That is convention, not tested evidence ([altrady](https://altrady.com/crypto-trading/technical-analysis/common-mistakes-candlestick-analysis)).

Your app may also offer **Heikin-Ashi** candles ("average bar" in Japanese). These are smoothed candles built from averages. The Heikin-Ashi close is the average of the period's open, high, low and close. The Heikin-Ashi open is the midpoint of the *previous* Heikin-Ashi candle's body ([BabyPips](https://www.babypips.com/learn/forex/how-to-calculate-heikin-ashi); [TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/support/solutions/43000619436)). Because of this smoothing, trends look cleaner, but the candles **do not show real open or close prices**, they hide gaps, and they flag trend changes late ([BabyPips](https://www.babypips.com/learn/forex/heikin-ashi-limitations)). If you use them at all, use them only to see trend direction. Any price you plan to actually trade at should come from regular candles. No study found here tests whether Heikin-Ashi improves results.

## Most named patterns test close to a coin flip

Candlestick patterns are named shapes, made of one, two or three candles, that tradition says signal a turn or a continuation. Every pattern's meaning depends on the trend before it. The same shape (small body near the top, long lower wick) is called a **hammer** after a decline and is supposed to be bullish. After a rally it is called a **hanging man** and is supposed to be bearish ([Fidelity/Bulkowski](https://www.fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/InvestmentCandles2.pdf)). The table below gives the common patterns, what tradition says, and what Thomas Bulkowski's large-sample testing found. Bulkowski treats anything under about 60% as essentially random ([Bulkowski](https://www.thepatternsite.com/CandleReverse.html)).

| Pattern | What it looks like | Traditional meaning | What testing found |
|---|---|---|---|
| Doji | Open and close nearly equal; a cross shape | Indecision; possible turn | Very common; meaning depends on context ([Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/learning-center/Idenitfying-Chart-Patterns.pdf)) |
| Hammer | Small body on top, long lower wick, after a decline | Bullish reversal | Reverses about 60% of the time; ranked 65th of 103 on performance ([Bulkowski](https://thepatternsite.com/Hammer.html)) |
| Hanging man | Same shape after a rally | Bearish reversal | Acted as a *continuation* 59% of the time ([Bulkowski](https://thepatternsite.com/HangingMan.html)) |
| Inverted hammer | Small body at bottom, long upper wick, after a decline | Bullish reversal | Acted as a *bearish continuation* 65% of the time ([Bulkowski](https://thepatternsite.com/HammerInv.html)) |
| Engulfing | Second candle's body fully covers the first's body | Reversal in the second candle's direction | Common starter pattern ([Fidelity/Bulkowski](https://www.fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/EightBestCandles.pdf)) |
| Bullish harami | Small body sitting inside the prior large body | Momentum fading | About 53%, near random ([ChartGuys citing Bulkowski](https://www.chartguys.com/articles/bullish-harami-pattern)) |
| Morning star | Big red candle, small "star", big green candle | Bullish reversal | Reverses 78% of the time; ranked 12th overall ([Bulkowski](https://thepatternsite.com/MorningStar.html)) |
| Bearish three-line strike | Three falling candles, then one big green candle | Bearish continuation | Acted as a *bullish reversal* 84% of the time ([Bulkowski](https://thepatternsite.com/ThreeLineStrikeBear.html)) |

Other names you will meet include the shooting star (a hanging-man cousin with a long upper wick after a rally), the marubozu (a full-body candle with no wicks, read as conviction), three white soldiers and three black crows (three strong candles in a row), and inside and outside bars (a candle whose range sits within, or swallows, the previous one). These are traditional readings, not statistical facts.

Academic tests are harsher still. Marshall, Young and Rose tested 28 candlestick signals on Dow Jones stocks from 1992 to 2002 against randomly generated price series. They found **"no evidence that any candlestick signals predict stock price movements"** and no evidence that a candlestick trader would beat the market ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/jbfina/v30y2006i8p2303-2323.html); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/candlesticks-fiddlesticks/)). The strongest counter-evidence is Caginalp and Laurent's study of S&P 500 stocks from 1992 to 1996. It found statistically significant predictive power in eight three-day reversal patterns, worth roughly 1% over a two-day hold ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:apmtfi:v:5:y:1998:i:3-4:p:181-205)). Lu, Chen and Hsu (2015) explain why the two studies disagree. Profitability depended mainly on **how the trade was exited**, not on the pattern. With one exit rule the three-day reversals survived 0.5% trading costs, and with another they did not. The edge was also stronger in volatile markets ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378426615002678)). Results outside the US are mixed. Some Taiwanese patterns were profitable after costs ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:pacfin:v:26:y:2014:i:c:p:65-78)), while China's SSE50 stocks showed no value at all ([SAGE Open](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440221117803)). Intraday candle patterns have even less academic support, and on short timeframes costs eat a larger share of each small move.

The one piece of context with solid data behind it is **location**. Bulkowski found that reversal candles work most often within a third of the stock's yearly high or low, and he advises ignoring reversal candles in the middle of the yearly range ([Bulkowski](https://www.thepatternsite.com/CandleReverse.html)). The popular advice to "wait for volume confirmation" of a candle has no candle-specific study behind it. Even for chart-pattern breakouts, Bulkowski found high volume less decisive than traders assume ([Bulkowski](https://thepatternsite.com/VolumeStudy.html)). The honest conclusion is to use candles to *describe* what buyers and sellers did, for example "sellers pushed it down and buyers bought it all back". Never treat a pattern name as a probability.

## A clean chart with four jobs beats a crowded one

Indicators are formulas applied to price and volume. Since most of them are built from the same recent closing prices, adding more of them mostly repeats the same information. That makes it easier to "confirm" whatever you already wanted to believe ([Tradeciety](https://www.tradeciety.com/how-to-choose-the-best-indicators-for-your-trading/)). Fidelity's beginner material starts with three tools: simple moving averages, MACD and Bollinger Bands. It groups indicators into trend, momentum and volume categories ([Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/learning-center/tech-indicator-2023-slides.pdf)). A better way to choose is by job. You need one tool for **trend**, one for **location** (where are the floors and ceilings?), one for **participation** (is anyone actually trading this?), and one for **risk**.

| Tool | Plain-English meaning | Typical use | Evidence quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Support / resistance | Prices where buying (a floor) or selling (a ceiling) has repeatedly appeared | Place stops just beyond them, targets near the next one | Small real effect: FX rates bounced at published levels 60.8% vs 56.2% at random levels ([NY Fed](https://resources.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)) |
| Moving averages (20 EMA, 50 & 200-day SMA) | Average closing price over N candles; the EMA weights recent candles more | Trend filter ("only buy above the 200-day"); pullback entry zone | Supported on long horizons and diversified portfolios; weak for short-term single-stock rules |
| VWAP | Today's average price paid, weighted by volume; resets each day | Intraday: above = buyers in control; pullbacks to VWAP | Only a working paper by authors who sell trading education (see below) |
| Volume / relative volume | Today's volume vs its normal level | Find "stocks in play"; check breakouts have participation | Relative-volume filter is the likely active ingredient in the ORB research |
| ATR (Average True Range) | Average size of a candle's range, including gaps | Set stop distance and position size | Not predictive by design; it is the most useful tool for managing risk |
| RSI, MACD, Bollinger Bands | How stretched or fast price is vs recent history | Context, divergence, "squeezes" | Mixed to negative after costs ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s40064-015-1334-7.pdf); [MPRA](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/54149/1/MPRA_paper_54149.pdf)) |

RSI, MACD and Bollinger Bands deserve one more word, because beginners lean on them hardest. RSI ranges from 0 to 100. Readings above 70 are labeled "overbought" and below 30 "oversold", but strong trends can sit "overbought" for weeks. MACD is a moving-average crossover in different clothing. Bollinger Bands, like RSI, measure how stretched price is. Stacking them counts the same evidence two or three times.

The long-run research arc tells you what to expect from all of these. Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron (1992) found that simple moving-average and range-breakout rules worked on the Dow from 1897 to 1986 ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:47:y:1992:i:5:p:1731-64)). Later work tested thousands of rules and corrected for the fact that if you try enough rules, one will look good by luck (called **data snooping**). It found that the edge faded, especially after 1986 ([Sullivan, Timmermann & White](https://bashtage.github.io/kevinsheppard.com/files/teaching/mfe/advanced-econometrics/Sullivan_Timmermann_White.pdf)). Bajgrowicz and Scaillet (2012) concluded that investors **could never have picked the winning rules in advance**, and that even the in-sample gains were "completely offset by the introduction of low transaction costs" ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:106:y:2012:i:3:p:473-491)). The robust survivor is slow, diversified **trend-following**, sometimes called time-series momentum. Buying what has risen over the past 12 months and selling what has fallen was profitable in all 58 futures markets studied ([Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463)) and across a century of data ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing)). That is a portfolio strategy measured in months, not a 5-minute chart skill. Its main lesson for you is that trading *with* the higher-timeframe trend is the best-supported default. Chart patterns themselves carry some information: head-and-shoulders and double tops shift the distribution of future returns. But the study that showed this never measured trading profits ([Lo, Mamaysky & Wang](https://web.mit.edu/~alo/www/Papers/techanal.html); [CXO Advisory](https://cxoadvisory.com/?p=987)).

For day trading, the most-cited recent evidence comes from Zarattini, Aziz and Barbon. Their opening-range breakout (ORB) strategy buys a break above the first 5 minutes' high, or shorts a break below its low, with the stop at the other side of the range. They restricted it to the **20 stocks with the highest relative volume** each day, and over 2016–2023 they reported a Sharpe ratio of 2.81 and more than 1,600% net return ([Concretum Group](https://concretumgroup.com/a-profitable-day-trading-strategy-for-the-u-s-equity-market/)). A companion paper claimed +671% from simply being long QQQ above VWAP and short below it ([Concretum Group](https://concretumgroup.com/volume-weighted-average-price-vwap-the-holy-grail-for-day-trading-systems/)). Read these with caution. They are unreviewed working papers, one author runs a trading-education business, and the VWAP paper calls itself "exploratory". An independent replication of index-level ORB reproduced the gross results but found **net results near zero** after costs ([MQL5 blog](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/776235)). The likely real ingredient is the "stocks in play" filter, meaning trading where news is driving unusual volume, rather than any particular line on the chart. That makes ORB a reasonable first strategy to *paper-test* on a bot, because its rules are precise. It is not a proven money-maker.

## The open is expensive, the close is deep, and night pays

Market activity has a **U-shape** across the day. Bid-ask spreads (the gap between the best price to buy and the best price to sell, which you pay on every round trip) are widest in the first minutes after the open and narrow quickly. Uncertainty is also highest early in the day and declines as the session goes on ([Chung et al.](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~keechung/limit%20orders.pdf); [Madhavan, Richardson & Roomans](https://archive.nyu.edu/handle/2451/27092)). Midday, roughly 11:30 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. ET, is the quietest, so breakouts then tend to have less volume behind them. The close is the deepest liquidity of the day. The closing auction alone handled about **9–10% of daily volume** in 2024–2026, and around 20% on options-expiration and index-rebalance days ([BMLL](https://www.bmlltech.com/news/market-insight/into-the-close-unpacking-u-s-closing-auction-dynamics-and-the-impact-of-the-russell-reconstitution); [NYSE](https://www.nyse.com/research/insights/behind-the-record-volumes-a-hidden-opportunity)). The practical upshot is that a beginner should **not place market orders in the first 15–30 minutes**. Wait for the opening range to form, use limit orders, and treat the close as a natural, liquid exit. Waiting 15–30 minutes is practitioner convention built on the spread evidence, not a separately tested edge, and the spread data are mostly from the 1990s.

Two timing effects have real research behind them. First, the market's direction from the prior close through 10:00 a.m. modestly predicts the direction of the **last half-hour** (3:30–4:00 p.m.). A rule of holding SPY only in the final half-hour after a positive morning earned about 6.3% a year *before costs*. The effect is small, with an out-of-sample R² of about 1.2%, meaning the morning explains only about 1% of the variation in the last half-hour's return ([Gao et al., EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:129:y:2018:i:2:p:394-414)). For a day trader already positioned in the morning's direction, it is a mild reason to exit into the close rather than at lunch. Second, **most index gains have historically come overnight**, from close to next open. From 1993 to 2006, overnight returns beat daytime returns for all 14 ETFs studied, and daytime returns were negative for 12 of them ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/calendar-effects/buy-at-the-close-and-sell-at-the-open)). Momentum profits in particular accrue overnight ([Lou, Polk & Skouras](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/polk/research/TugOfWar.pdf)). This is the core structural trade-off between the two styles. A day trader who is always flat by 4:00 p.m. gives up the period that historically paid most. A swing trader collects it but accepts **gap risk**: earnings and big news usually land outside market hours, and the price can open far past a stop.

| | Day trade | Swing trade |
|---|---|---|
| Holding period | Minutes to hours, flat by the close | Days to weeks |
| Chart to decide direction | Daily (trend, prior day high/low) | Weekly and daily |
| Chart to time entry | 1–5 minute, with VWAP | Daily, sometimes hourly |
| Best entry window | After the first 15–30 minutes; avoid midday chop | Near the close or the next session, once a daily candle has closed |
| Exit | Stop, target, or the close | Stop (ATR-based), trailing stop, target, or time stop |
| Main risk | Costs and noise eat small edges | Overnight and earnings gaps jump past stops |
| What the evidence says | Fewer than 1% reliably profitable ([Barber et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:1-24)) | Captures the overnight return; fewer trades mean lower costs |

Extended hours (pre-market and after-hours) have thinner trading, wider spreads, sometimes no quotes at all, and bigger swings. The SEC and FINRA both warn about this ([Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletin-after-hours-trading); [FINRA Rule 2265](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/2265)), so if you trade then at all, use limit orders only. Calendar folklore deserves less weight. The "Monday effect" was gone by the early 1980s ([ASU](https://news.asu.edu/20170201-discoveries-asu-research-debunks-myth-stock-market-weekend-effect)). The turn-of-the-month effect (last trading day plus the first three) was strong through 2005 ([McConnell & Xu](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:ufajxx:v:64:y:2008:i:2:p:49-64)) but has no confirmed recent evidence. The day before Federal Reserve announcements earned about 49 basis points (0.49%) on average from 1994 to 2011 ([Lucca & Moench](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f64395/f64395.pdf)), and no rigorous post-2011 update was found. None of these justifies picking a single-stock entry date.

Entry *style* is unsettled. No rigorous study shows that breakout entries (buying as price clears a level) beat pullback entries (buying a dip back toward a rising average or support), or the reverse. Pullbacks give a closer stop and a better price. Breakouts make sure you are in a strong move. Waiting for a candle to *close* beyond a level filters out some false breakouts at the cost of a worse fill. Pick one style per strategy and test it. No outside rule makes the choice for you.

## Exits and position size decide whether you survive

A **stop-loss** is a pre-set exit if price moves against you. The research on stops is conditional. If prices moved randomly, stops would always lower expected returns. When prices trend, stops can raise returns while cutting volatility ([Kaminski & Lo](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:234-254)). In one study, a simple 10% stop on a momentum portfolio cut the worst monthly loss from about −49.8% to about −11.4% and roughly doubled the Sharpe ratio ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2016/08/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/)). Reviewers caution that the stop level may have been tuned after the fact, and that real losses exceed the stop when price gaps past it ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/stop-losses-to-avoid-stock-momentum-crashes/)). So stops belong on trend and breakout trades. On "buy the dip" mean-reversion trades, a tight stop tends to sell at the worst moment. Placing the stop a multiple of ATR away (commonly 1.5–3×) adapts to each stock's normal noise better than a fixed percentage. That is practitioner convention, not tested.

The best-documented exit mistake is behavioral. Investors sell winners too early and hold losers too long, a pattern called the **disposition effect**. In 10,000 brokerage accounts this habit lowered returns ([Odean](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/disposition/disposition.html)). Writing your exits down before you enter, and letting a bot execute them, is the most direct defense. Simple arithmetic also helps you set targets. A trade that risks $1 to make $2 (a 1:2 risk/reward) breaks even only if it wins more than one time in three (1 ÷ 3 ≈ 33%), before costs. Trailing stops, partial profit-taking and time stops (exit if nothing has happened within N candles or days) are reasonable rules, but none has been academically tested.

Position size matters more than any of the tools above. Position size is the amount you risk divided by the distance from entry to stop. Risking 0.5–1% of the account per trade means 10 straight losses at 1% still leave about 90% of the account. That is a survival rule of thumb, not a researched optimum. The outcome data are sobering. In Taiwan, day traders as a group lost money net of fees **in every one of 15 years**, and fewer than 1% earned predictable profits ([Barber et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:1-24); [preprint](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/day%20traders/day%20trading%20and%20learning%20110217.pdf)). Among Brazilians who day-traded futures for more than 300 days, **97% lost money**, with no sign of improving over time ([Chague et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:spa:wpaper:2019wpecon47)). Note also that in 2026 FINRA eliminated the Pattern Day Trader rule and its $25,000 minimum, replacing them with intraday margin requirements ([SEC order](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/finra/2026/34-104572.pdf)). Most sources give June 4, 2026 as the effective date, but some firms have a phase-in period ([TradeZero](https://tradezero.com/en-us/blog/how-intraday-margin-replaced-the-pattern-day-trading-rule); [SoFi](https://www.sofi.com/learn/content/pattern-day-trading-rules/)), so confirm how Alpaca applies it to your account. Losing the old three-day-trades-per-week guardrail makes a self-imposed limit on trade count, coded into your bot, more important, not less.

## Conclusion

The research reverses the usual beginner priorities. Pattern recognition, the part that feels like skill, has the weakest evidence. Exit rules, position size, trading with the higher-timeframe trend, and staying away from the open's wide spreads have the strongest. Even the best candle studies found that the *exit rule* mattered more than the pattern, so a bot's real value is not spotting hammers. It is executing a fixed exit and size every time, without the disposition effect getting in the way. Treat every backtest, including the impressive ORB and VWAP papers and your own paper-trading results, as an upper bound. The documented pattern is that edges which look strong before costs shrink or vanish after costs and after correcting for how many rules were tried.

For a beginner, the trade-off between day trading and swing trading is not close on the evidence. Swing trading on daily charts trades less, pays fewer spreads, and collects the overnight return that has historically carried the market. Day trading fights a base rate in which fewer than 1% succeed. A sensible path is to start with swing rules on the daily chart, paper-trade them long enough to log at least dozens of trades, and add an intraday strategy (such as an opening-range breakout on high-relative-volume stocks) only as a separate, small, measured experiment.

## Beginner checklist

**Reading a chart**

1. Check your app's settings: does the candle color compare close to open or to the prior close, and do daily candles include extended hours?
2. Start on the weekly chart, then the daily. Is price above or below its 50-day and 200-day moving averages? That is your trend.
3. Mark the obvious floors and ceilings (support and resistance): recent swing highs and lows, the prior day's high and low, and the 52-week high and low.
4. Note where price sits in its yearly range. Ignore reversal candles in the middle of the range.
5. Read the last few candles as a story (who pushed, who won by the close), not as a named signal. Never act on a candle that has not closed yet.
6. Check volume and relative volume. Is today unusual, and is there news?
7. Limit the chart to price, volume, one or two moving averages, VWAP (intraday only) and ATR. Remove any indicator that repeats another.

**Entering a trade**

1. Write the plan before you enter: the setup, the entry price, the stop (where you are proven wrong) and the target.
2. Trade in the direction of the higher-timeframe trend unless your strategy is explicitly built to do otherwise.
3. Size the position as (account × 0.5–1%) ÷ (entry − stop). Use ATR to make sure the stop sits outside normal noise.
4. Confirm the risk/reward clears your break-even win rate (1:2 needs better than 33%).
5. Day trades: skip the first 15–30 minutes, use limit orders, prefer stocks in play, and be wary of midday breakouts.
6. Swing trades: enter after a daily candle closes, and check the earnings date. Holding through earnings means accepting a gap your stop cannot stop.
7. Never use market orders in pre-market or after-hours trading.

**Exiting a trade**

1. Place the stop at entry, inside the bot, not in your head.
2. Exit by rule: stop, target, trailing stop, or time stop. Do not move a stop further away.
3. Day trades: be flat by the close. The closing auction is the most liquid exit.
4. Watch for the disposition effect. If you want to hold a loser "until it comes back", that is the bias talking.
5. Log every trade, including costs and slippage, and compare paper results with what live fills would have been before risking real money.
6. Cap the number of trades per day or week in the bot's code, especially now that the PDT limit is gone.
