# Own the Market, Then Test Your Bots

For a US beginner in late 2026, the best-supported plan is boring on purpose. Put the large majority of your money into a cheap, diversified index fund portfolio inside the right tax accounts. Treat stock picking, day trading, options buying and crypto bots as a small, separately measured experiment that has to prove it can beat that portfolio. The evidence behind this is lopsided. **79% of professional US large-cap stock funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025**. About **4% of all US stocks produced all of the market's net wealth creation since 1926**. **Fewer than 1% of day traders** in the largest study reliably made money after fees. And the most active individual investors earned about **11.4% a year when the market earned 17.9%**. Several rules changed in 2026. The $25,000 "pattern day trader" minimum is gone (effective June 4, 2026, with brokers allowed to phase it in until October 2027). Retirement-account limits and capital-gains brackets rose. 23-hour weekday stock trading is targeted for December 6, 2026 but is not yet live. For your Alpaca setup, this means three things. Run the bots on paper first. Size every trade so a long losing streak cannot hurt you badly. Judge each bot against a "shadow" account that simply holds SPY (an S&P 500 index fund), after realistic costs and taxes, and over hundreds of trades rather than a few good weeks. The guide below explains the mechanics, the evidence and the rules of thumb, and ends with a step-by-step checklist.

**How to read this guide.** Claims are tagged as **(evidence)** when they come from peer-reviewed studies, official data or regulation, and as **(rule of thumb)** when they are practitioner convention without rigorous testing behind them. Unlabeled arithmetic is just math. Where sources disagree, the guide says so. This is education, not personal tax or investment advice. Check the tax figures against IRS documents or with a tax professional before acting on them.

## A share is a slice of a company, priced by an auction that settles next day

A **share** (also called a stock) is a small piece of ownership in a company. **Common stock** usually carries a vote and a claim on profits, but its dividends are never guaranteed. **Preferred stock** usually has no vote, pays a set dividend that comes before common dividends, and ranks ahead of common holders if the company is wound up ([SEC Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/stocks)). When you buy a share, someone else is selling it. Trades happen on exchanges such as the NYSE and Nasdaq, and also off-exchange with **market makers**, firms that stand ready to buy and sell all day.

Every stock has two live prices. The **bid** is the highest price a buyer will currently pay, and the **ask** is the lowest price a seller will accept. The gap between them is the **spread**, and you pay roughly half of it each time you trade with a market order. Market makers earn the spread. Spreads are widest in thinly traded stocks and outside regular hours ([SEC glossary](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary)). "Commission-free" brokers make much of their money from **payment for order flow (PFOF)**: market makers pay the broker to send them retail orders. PFOF is legal if disclosed, and brokers must publish routing reports under SEC Rules 605 and 606 ([SoGoTrade Rule 606/607 disclosure](https://content.sogotrade.com/PDF/en-us/sogotrade_SEC_Rules_606_607.pdf)). The current SEC withdrew the proposed "order competition rule" that targeted PFOF, and brokers' duty to get you the best execution is unchanged ([Global Trading](https://www.globaltrading.net/?p=38776)). The practical point is that your trading cost does not show up as a fee. It hides in the price you get filled at, which is why a bot that trades often needs to log its real fills.

**Regular hours** run 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time on weekdays. Most brokers also offer pre-market (from about 4:00 a.m.) and after-hours (to about 8:00 p.m.) sessions, which have less trading, wider spreads and bigger swings ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/extended-hours-trading)). That is changing. The SEC approved Nasdaq's plan for **23-hour weekday trading** on April 10, 2026, with an overnight session from 9 p.m. to 4 a.m. ET. Nasdaq and NYSE Arca both target **December 6, 2026** to launch, but as of late October several supporting rules and market-data readiness were still pending ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/04/sec-approves-nasdaq-proposal-to-expand-trading-hours); [Harvard Law School Forum](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/10/21/nyse-arca-and-nasdaq-to-launch-extended-trading-what-companies-should-consider/)). Expect overnight trading, if it launches, to behave like today's extended hours: thin and expensive. A bot should use limit orders only in those sessions, or skip them.

**Settlement** is the back-office step when cash and shares actually change hands. Since May 28, 2024, US stocks, ETFs and options settle **T+1**, one business day after the trade ([Robinhood support](https://robinhood.com/us/en/support/articles/T1-settlements)). Settlement matters for cash accounts and for dividends, both covered below.

### Order types decide whether you control the price or the fill

An **order type** is the instruction you give the broker. The core trade-off is simple. A market order guarantees you get filled but not the price. A limit order guarantees the price but not that you get filled ([SEC Investor.gov, Types of Orders](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/types-orders)).

| Order type | What it does | Main risk | When a beginner should use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | Buy or sell now at the best available price | Bad fill in fast markets, at the open, in thin stocks | Liquid stocks and ETFs during regular hours, small size |
| Limit | Buy at or below (sell at or above) your price | May never fill | Default for bots; required in extended hours |
| Stop (stop-loss) | Becomes a market order when the trigger price trades | Overnight gaps can fill you far below the stop | Protective exit on trend and breakout trades |
| Stop-limit | Becomes a limit order at the trigger | May not fill at all if price jumps past your limit | When a bad fill is worse than no fill |
| Trailing stop | Stop that rises with the price by a set $ or % ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/trading-101-understanding-order-types)) | Same gap risk as a stop | Letting a winner run with a moving exit |
| Bracket | Entry plus a profit-target limit plus a protective stop | Broker feature, not a regulated term | Making every bot trade carry its exits from the start |

**Time in force** sets how long an order lives. A **day** order expires at the close. A **good-'til-canceled (GTC)** order stays open until filled or canceled, within the broker's limit. **Fractional shares** let you buy a dollar amount, such as $10 of a $500 stock. They may not transfer to another broker ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/fractional-share-investing-buy-bit-stock)). Two practical points for your bots. A stop-loss does not guarantee your exit price (evidence: the SEC's own warning above). And on Alpaca, crypto does not support bracket or trailing-stop orders, so a crypto bot must run its own exits in code ([Alpaca Forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/is-it-possible-to-make-a-bracket-order-for-crypto/8085)).

### Funds, indexes and fees

A **single stock** is one company. An **index** is a rulebook for a list of stocks. The **S&P 500** holds about 500 large US companies, weighted by market value. The **Dow Jones Industrial Average** holds 30 stocks, weighted by share price. The **Nasdaq-100** holds the 100 largest non-financial Nasdaq companies. The **Russell 2000** tracks small companies ([S&P Dow Jones Indices](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/)). An **index fund** simply owns everything in an index. It comes in two wrappers. A **mutual fund** prices once a day, after the close, at its **net asset value (NAV)**, the value of its holdings per share. An **ETF (exchange-traded fund)** trades all day like a stock and is usually more tax-efficient ([SEC Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-funds-etfs)). The **expense ratio** is the annual fee as a percentage of your money. Costs are at record lows: the asset-weighted average was about **0.32% for all US funds and about 0.10% for index funds in 2025** (evidence: [Morningstar Fund Fee Study 2026](https://www.morningstar.com/content/cs-assets/v3/assets/blt9415ea4cc4157833/bltceffe0d68d048b0a/6a076fcbc9bda1fb11c9f0a6/US_Fund_Fee_Study_2026.pdf)). A broad index ETF at 0.03–0.10% costs $3–$10 a year per $10,000.

Stocks are also grouped by **market capitalization** ("market cap," share price times shares outstanding). Rough cutoffs (rule of thumb; index providers set their own) are large-cap above about $10 billion, mid-cap $2–10 billion and small-cap $250 million–$2 billion. Stocks are also grouped by **sector**. The standard GICS system has 11 sectors, from Information Technology to Utilities. In 2023 Visa and Mastercard were moved from Tech into Financials ([Marquette Associates](https://www.marquetteassociates.com/gics-reclassifies-away-tech/)), a reminder that sector labels are conventions, not laws of nature.

Companies also take actions that change your share count. A **stock split** (for example 2-for-1) doubles your shares and halves the price, leaving your value unchanged. A **reverse split** (for example 1-for-10) is often used to keep a falling stock above an exchange's $1 minimum, and in small companies it is frequently a warning sign ([SEC Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/stock-split)). **Buybacks** shrink the share count, and since 2023 they carry a 1% federal excise tax ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-and-treasury-issue-guidance-on-the-excise-tax-on-repurchases-of-corporate-stock)). Every public company's filings are free on [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/): the **10-K** (audited annual report), the **10-Q** (unaudited quarterly report) and the **8-K** (news such as earnings, within four business days) ([SEC Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/how-read)).

### What "normal" returns look like

US large-cap stocks have returned **about 10% a year since 1926**, before inflation, with about 73 up years and 26 down years through 2024 (evidence: [Dimensional](https://my.dimensional.com/chmedia/333079/source/the-bumpy-road-to-the-markets-long-term-average.pdf)). The average hides the ride. Only six calendar years since 1926 actually landed between 8% and 12% ([Hartford Funds](https://www.hartfordfunds.com/dam/en/docs/pub/whitepapers/CCWP091.pdf)). There were 18 **bear markets** (falls of 20% or more from a peak), ranging from −21% to −80% ([Dimensional](https://dimensional.com/dfsmedia/f27f1cc5b9674653938eb84ff8006d8c/39602-source/bulls-bears-and-long-term-benefits-of-stock-investing.pdf)). On average, a bear market fell about a third and took about 21 months to regain its old peak ([A Wealth of Common Sense](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2025/05/60-40-portfolio-corrections-bear-markets-and-recoveries/)). Fees, taxes and inflation all come out of that 10%. The number to plan around is lower, and the path is bumpy.

## The 2026 rulebook: accounts, the end of the $25,000 rule, and taxes

### Pick the account before you pick the stock

The account you hold an investment in can matter more than the investment itself, because of taxes. The 2026 limits are below (evidence: IRS Notice 2025-67 via [InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/irs-reveals-new-401k-ira-contribution-limits-for-2026/263029) and [PSCA](https://www.psca.org/news/psca-news/2025/11/irs-announces-2026-401k-contribution-limits/)).

| Account | What it is | 2026 limit | Key detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| 401(k) / 403(b) / TSP | Employer retirement plan | $24,500 (was $23,500) | +$8,000 catch-up at 50+; +$11,250 at ages 60–63 |
| Traditional IRA | Individual retirement account; contributions may be tax-deductible, withdrawals taxed as income | $7,500 (was $7,000) | +$1,100 catch-up at 50+ |
| Roth IRA | After-tax contributions; qualified withdrawals tax-free | Same $7,500 shared with Traditional | Income phase-out $153k–$168k single, $242k–$252k married filing jointly |
| Taxable brokerage | Ordinary account (your Alpaca account) | No limit | Gains and dividends taxed yearly |

Inside an IRA or 401(k), trades and dividends are not taxed year by year ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/traditional-and-roth-iras)). A common priority order (rule of thumb) is: take any 401(k) employer match first, then fund an IRA (Roth if eligible), then use a taxable account.

Brokerage accounts come in two kinds. A **cash account** requires you to pay in full. A **margin account** lets you borrow from the broker. Under Regulation T you can borrow up to 50% of a stock purchase. FINRA requires at least $2,000 of equity and at least 25% "maintenance margin." If your equity falls below that, you get a **margin call** and may be force-sold ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/margin-accounts); [Alpaca](https://alpaca.markets/learn/what-is-margin-trading)). Margin magnifies losses exactly as much as gains. A beginner gets no benefit from it, and options purchases generally must be paid in full anyway. **SIPC** insurance covers up to $500,000 per customer (including up to $250,000 cash) if your **broker fails**. It does not cover market losses ([CRS report RS21741](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RS21741.html)).

### The pattern day trader rule is gone, but guardrails remain

Until mid-2026, anyone who made four or more day trades in five business days in a margin account was labeled a **pattern day trader** and needed $25,000 of equity. FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-10 **replaced that rule "in their entirety" with an intraday margin standard, effective June 4, 2026** (evidence, regulation: [FINRA RN 26-10](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/26-10); [SEC release 34-105226](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/finra/2026/34-105226.pdf)). Under the new standard, your broker measures whether your account fell below its required margin at any point during the day, not just at the close. A shortfall must be covered by a deposit or by selling positions, and repeated failures can bring a 90-day restriction ([King & Spalding](https://www.kslaw.com/news-and-insights/finra-adopts-sweeping-changes-to-margin-requirements-for-day-trading)). Two cautions. Brokers may keep operating under the old rules during a **transition period running to October 20, 2027**. And brokers may block trades in real time once your intraday buying power is used up. Broker summaries also describe the details differently ([TradeZero](https://tradezero.com/en-us/blog/the-usd25-000-day-trading-minimum-is-gone-here-s-what-it-means-for-you); [QuantInsti](https://www.quantinsti.com/articles/finra-pdt-rule-removal-2026/)). Check Alpaca's current policy for your account before assuming you can day trade freely. Many guides written before 2026 still describe the $25,000 rule. They are out of date.

**Cash accounts** were never subject to the PDT rule, but they are bound by settlement. If you buy a stock and sell it before you have paid for it, that is **freeriding**. It can freeze the account for 90 days, during which you must pay in full on the trade date (evidence, regulation: [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/general-resources/glossary/freeriding); [SEC](https://www.sec.gov/investor/alerts/cashaccounts.pdf)). If you buy with unsettled sale proceeds and then sell the new position before those proceeds settle, that is a **good-faith violation**. Brokers typically restrict the account after three or four of these in 12 months ([Walnut](https://walnutinvest.com/help/what-is-a-good-faith-violation); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeriding_(stocks))). A day-trading bot in a cash account must therefore track **settled cash**, not just account balance.

### Taxes reward patience and punish churn

The single most important tax fact for a trader is the one-year line. Gains on assets held **one year or less are short-term** and taxed as ordinary income at 10% to 37%. Gains on assets held **more than one year are long-term** and taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% (evidence: [IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409)). The 2025 tax law (OBBBA) made the current ordinary rate brackets permanent ([ustax.tools](https://ustax.tools/capital-gains-tax-rates-2026/)). The 2026 long-term thresholds, based on taxable income, are below. They come from secondary summaries of IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 ([VisaVerge](https://www.visaverge.com/taxes/0-capital-gains-bracket-2026-thresholds-in-revenue-procedure-2025-32/); [Current Federal Tax Developments](https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2025/10/9/2026-inflation-adjustments-for-tax-professionals-revenue-procedure-2025-32-analysis)). One site lists slightly different figures, so verify against the IRS text.

| Filing status | 0% rate up to | 15% rate up to | 20% rate above |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single | $49,450 | $545,500 | $545,500 |
| Married filing jointly | $98,900 | $613,700 | $613,700 |
| Head of household | $66,200 | $579,600 | $579,600 |

On top of this, a 3.8% **Net Investment Income Tax** applies above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (joint) of income. Those thresholds are not adjusted for inflation ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/net-investment-income-tax)). Here is what the line means in practice. A $1,000 gain for someone in the 22% bracket costs $220 in tax if sold at 364 days and $150 if sold at 366 days. Every profitable bot trade is short-term, so a bot has to beat buy-and-hold by enough to cover that extra tax as well. A bot's profits look very different inside an IRA, where no tax is due each year.

Three more rules matter. The **wash-sale rule** disallows a loss if you buy "substantially identical" stock within 30 days before or after the sale. The loss is not gone forever: it is added to the cost of the new shares ([Interactive Brokers](https://ibkb.interactivebrokers.com/hu/node/929); [TaxAct](https://www.taxact.com/support/602)). Buying the same stock in your IRA can trigger it too ([IRS Pub 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550)). A bot that repeatedly trades the same ticker at a loss will generate wash sales constantly, so its tax losses may be deferred rather than usable. **Tax-loss harvesting** means selling losers to offset gains. Net losses can offset up to $3,000 a year of ordinary income, and the rest carries forward ([IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409)). Finally, brokers report every sale on **Form 1099-B**, which feeds Form 8949 and Schedule D ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1099-b)). A busy bot can produce hundreds of lines. Crypto is reported separately on the new Form 1099-DA, and the wash-sale rule still did not apply to crypto held directly as of this research ([Harness](https://www.harness.co/articles/new-1099-da-broker-reporting-rules-for-2026-what-crypto-investors-need-to-know); [TurboTax](https://ttlc.intuit.com/turbotax-support/en-us/help-article/cryptocurrency/wash-sale-rule-cryptocurrency/L1d6BuQpH_US_en_US)). State income tax comes on top of all of this in most states.

| What changed (2025–2026) | Status as of Nov 4, 2026 |
|---|---|
| PDT rule and $25,000 minimum replaced by intraday margin | In effect June 4, 2026; brokers may phase in until Oct 20, 2027 |
| 23-hour weekday trading (Nasdaq, NYSE Arca) | Approved; targeted for Dec 6, 2026; not yet live |
| IRA limit $7,500; 401(k) $24,500 | In effect for 2026 |
| Capital-gains brackets indexed up | In effect for 2026 |
| TCJA ordinary rates made permanent (OBBBA) | In effect |
| REIT 20% dividend deduction made permanent (OBBBA) | In effect ([Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/client-alerts/reit-all-about-it-one-big-beautiful-bill-tax-updates-for-reits)) |
| SEC order-competition rule targeting PFOF | Withdrawn |

## Index funds win by default because stock returns are lopsided

### The base rate: most stock pickers lose to the index

Before learning how to pick stocks, a beginner should see the scoreboard. **79% of actively managed US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025**, the fourth-worst year in the scorecard's 25-year history (evidence: [S&P SPIVA US Year-End 2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-us-year-end-2025/)). In the first half of 2026, as small and value stocks rallied, the figure improved to **67%** ([SPIVA US Mid-Year 2026](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/spiva-us-mid-year-2026.pdf)). Over longer windows it gets worse: about 84% lagged over 5 years, 90% over 10 years and 95% over 20 years, as of mid-2022 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/10/01/underperformance-is-abysmal-in-the-long-run-for-active-fund-managers.html)). Winners also rarely stay winners. Only 29% of top-quarter funds in 2023 were still top-quarter two years later ([SPIVA Persistence](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/us-persistence-scorecard/)). Morningstar's survivorship-adjusted data agree: only 37% of active US stock funds beat their passive peers in 2025. Over 10 years, the cheapest active funds succeeded 31% of the time and the priciest only 17% ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/funds/better-conditions-did-not-yield-better-results-active-managers-2025)). A 2026 academic study argues that SPIVA overstates the gap ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/05/05/3287720/0/en/Active-Management-Performs-Much-Better-Than-SPIVA-U-S-Scorecard-States-Finds-New-Academic-Study.html)). Even granting that, no serious source claims most active managers win.

The deeper reason is that stock returns are **lopsided**, or "positively skewed." Hendrik Bessembinder studied about 26,000 US stocks from 1926 to 2016. He found that just **1,092 firms, about 4%, produced all of the roughly $35 trillion of net wealth the market created above Treasury bills**. Just 90 firms produced more than half. **58% of individual stocks failed to beat one-month Treasury bills over their lifetimes** (evidence: [Bessembinder, JFE 2018](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/sites/default/files/2021-10/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills.pdf)). That means the median stock lags the index. A random ten-stock portfolio will lag the market more often than it beats it, because it will usually miss the rare giant winners. Owning the whole market guarantees you own them.

Individual investors do worse still, mostly because they trade too much. In Barber and Odean's study of 66,465 households from 1991 to 1996, the average household earned 16.4% a year while the market earned 17.9%. **The 20% who traded most earned just 11.4%**. Costs and trading frequency, not bad stock choices, explained the gap. The authors blame overconfidence (evidence: [Barber & Odean, Journal of Finance 2000](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/Papers%20current%20versions/Individual_Investor_Performance_Final.pdf)). In a follow-up, men traded 45% more than women and paid for it in returns ([Barber & Odean, "Boys Will Be Boys"](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/gender/gender.html)). Commissions are now zero, but spreads, short-term taxes and overtrading still cost money, so the "trade less" lesson still applies.

### If you do pick stocks: the fundamental analysis toolkit

**Fundamental analysis** means judging a company by its business and finances rather than its chart. It starts with three linked statements. The **income statement** shows revenue, costs and profit over a period. The **balance sheet** shows what the company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities) on one date; the difference is shareholders' **equity**. The **cash flow statement** shows actual cash coming in and going out ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/how-to-invest/stocks/beginners-guide-financial-statements); [Investing.com Academy](https://www.investing.com/academy/analysis/how-to-read-financial-statements-for-investing/)). The SEC publishes a free [Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements](https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/beginners-guide-financial-statements). The key metrics below are standard textbook definitions. The "watch for" column is rule of thumb.

| Metric | Formula | What it tells you | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth | This year's revenue ÷ last year's − 1 | Is the business getting bigger? | Growth bought through acquisitions |
| Gross / operating / net margin | Gross profit, operating income or net income ÷ revenue | How much of each sales dollar is kept | Falling margins |
| EPS (earnings per share) | Net income ÷ diluted shares | Profit per share | Buybacks can raise EPS with no profit growth |
| Free cash flow (FCF) | Operating cash flow − capital spending | Real cash the business throws off | Net income persistently above operating cash flow |
| Debt/equity | Total debt ÷ equity | How much the firm borrows | Misleading when buybacks shrink equity |
| ROE / ROIC | Profit ÷ equity; after-tax operating profit ÷ invested capital | How well the firm uses money | ROE inflated by debt; prefer ROIC |

Profitability is one of the few fundamentals with strong research behind it. Novy-Marx (2013) found that **gross profit divided by assets predicts returns about as well as cheapness does** (evidence: [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/profitability/)).

**Valuation** asks whether the price is sensible. The most common measure is the **P/E ratio**, price divided by earnings per share. "Trailing" P/E uses the last 12 months of earnings. "Forward" P/E uses analysts' estimates, which tend to be optimistic. Other measures are P/S (price to sales, useful for unprofitable firms), P/B (price to book value, useful for banks), EV/EBITDA (which accounts for debt) and FCF yield. A **discounted cash flow (DCF)** model values a company as the present value of its future cash. Small changes in its assumptions swing the answer a lot, so treat it as a sensitivity exercise, not a precise number. For context, the S&P 500 traded at about **18.7 times forward earnings in late October 2026**, slightly below its 5-year (19.8) and 10-year (19.1) averages, after sitting above average at 20.0 in July (evidence: [FactSet](https://insight.factset.com/sp-500-earnings-season-update-october-30-2026?hs_amp=true); [FactSet July](https://advantage.factset.com/hubfs/Website/Resources%20Section/Research%20Desk/Earnings%20Insight/EarningsInsight_070226.pdf)). "Cheap" always means cheap relative to growth, risk and quality. Low-multiple stocks are often cheap for a reason, which is called a **value trap**.

Qualitative analysis asks whether the business has a **moat**, a durable advantage against competitors. Morningstar's framework names five sources: intangible assets (brands, patents), switching costs, network effects, cost advantage and efficient scale (opinion/framework: [Morningstar](https://hk.morningstar.com/ODS_Images/Mstar_MoatRating.pdf)). A moat helps only if the price doesn't already reflect it. Analyst "Buy" ratings deserve skepticism. Following the top consensus picks once earned abnormal returns before costs, but the high turnover needed to capture them ate the edge ([Barber et al., Journal of Finance 2001](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:56:y:2001:i:2:p:531-563)). In 2000–2001 analysts' favorites actually did worse than their pans ([Barber et al., 2003](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v59y2003i2p88-96.html)). Brokerages also skew heavily toward Buy ratings.

Two research findings directly affect an earnings-trading bot. First, the classic **post-earnings drift**, where stocks keep moving in the direction of an earnings surprise, has been **essentially gone for large US stocks since about 2006** (evidence: [Quantpedia summary of Martineau 2021](https://quantpedia.com/50-years-in-pead-research/); [Zhao paper](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/imce-uploads/CEASA/Events%20Page/PEAD_Declined_over_time.pdf)). Second, published stock-market patterns fade once people know about them. Across 97 published predictors, returns were **58% lower after publication** (evidence: [McLean & Pontiff, Journal of Finance 2016](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf)). Broad "factors" such as value, small size, momentum, profitability and low volatility have long-run premiums, but they can lag for a decade. The value factor fell about **55% from 2007 to 2020**, its worst drawdown on record ([Research Affiliates](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/insights/journal-papers/reports-of-values-death-may-be-greatly-exaggerated)), before rebounding strongly in 2026 ([SSGA](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/individual/insights/mind-on-the-market-27-july-2026)). Expect any strategy you read about to deliver much less than its backtest.

The verdict: picking stocks is a legitimate hobby and a way to learn, but the evidence says it should live in a small "satellite" sleeve. Write a short thesis before each purchase (why this company, what price, what would make you sell). Keep any single stock to a small slice of the total.

## Charts describe what happened; rules and position size do the work

The earlier report in this series, *Reading candlestick charts and trade timing*, covers chart reading in depth. Here is the short version. A **candlestick** shows four prices for one period: open, high, low and close. The thick **body** spans open to close, and thin **wicks** mark the extremes. Green means it closed above its own open, which is not the same as being up on the day. Rigorous tests of named candle patterns mostly find **no reliable edge**. Marshall, Young and Rose found "no evidence that any candlestick signals predict stock price movements" on Dow stocks ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/jbfina/v30y2006i8p2303-2323.html)). Thomas Bulkowski's large-sample tests found several famous patterns near a coin flip, and some behaving opposite to their textbook meaning ([Bulkowski](https://www.thepatternsite.com/CandleReverse.html)). Simple technical rules that once worked faded after costs and after correcting for the number of rules tried ([Bajgrowicz & Scaillet](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:106:y:2012:i:3:p:473-491)). The best-supported survivor is slow **trend-following** over months, across many markets ([Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463)). So "trade in the direction of the bigger trend" is the most defensible chart rule.

The useful chart tools tell you **where you are wrong and how much to risk**, rather than predicting. These include support and resistance levels, the 50- and 200-day moving averages as trend filters, relative volume, and **ATR (average true range)**, the typical size of a candle including gaps. On timing, the evidence is clearer. Spreads are widest in the **first 15–30 minutes after the 9:30 open**. The closing auction is the deepest liquidity of the day. Historically **most index gains came overnight**, between the close and the next open, which favors swing trades over strictly intraday ones ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/calendar-effects/buy-at-the-close-and-sell-at-the-open)). The opening-range-breakout strategy is a reasonable first day-trading rule set to paper test, because its rules are precise. Its headline results come from unreviewed working papers, and an independent replication found net results near zero after costs. For full detail, including indicator tables and entry and exit checklists, see the earlier report.

## Day trading, swing trading and options buying: styles, risk and the odds

### Four styles, four time commitments

The conventional styles below are practitioner definitions (rule of thumb).

| Style | Typical hold | Time needed | Main cost or risk | Tax result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Day trading | Minutes to hours; flat by the close | Full attention during market hours (or a bot) | Spreads and fees on every trade; noise | All short-term |
| Swing trading | Days to a few weeks | About an hour a day | Overnight and earnings gaps jump past stops | All short-term |
| Position trading | Weeks to months | Weekly review | Larger drawdowns; trend changes | Mostly short-term |
| Buy-and-hold investing | Years | Minimal | Bear markets; your own panic | Long-term (lower rates) |

The shorter the hold, the more trades you make, and the more spreads, slippage and short-term tax you pay. The longer the hold, the more overnight **gap risk** you carry: earnings and news usually land outside market hours, and a stock can open far beyond your stop.

### The evidence on outcomes is grim for day traders and option buyers

The largest study of day traders covered Taiwan from 1992 to 2006. **Fewer than 1% of day traders predictably and reliably earned positive returns net of fees**. As a group, they lost about 0.29% per day after fees (evidence: [Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, Journal of Financial Markets 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v18y2014icp1-24.html)). More than 8 in 10 lost money in a typical six-month period ([Barber et al.](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt7k75v0qx/qt7k75v0qx.pdf?t=ohrd1z)). In Brazil, among everyone who began day trading index futures in 2013–2015 and kept at it for more than 300 days, **97% lost money**, and only about 0.4% earned more than a bank teller's wage (evidence: [Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101)). Persistence did not mean learning. No comparable large US study was found, but nothing suggests US retail traders do better.

Because you plan to buy calls and puts, the options evidence matters most. A **call** is a contract giving the right to buy 100 shares at a set **strike price** before an **expiration** date. A **put** gives the right to sell. When you buy one you pay a **premium**, and that premium is the most you can lose ([SEC](https://sec.gov/oiea/investor-alerts-bulletins/ib_introductionoptions)). Three forces work against the buyer. **Theta** (time decay) shrinks the option's value every day, and the decay speeds up as expiration nears. **Implied volatility (IV)**, the market's priced-in expectation of future movement, collapses after events like earnings. That "IV crush" can cut an option's price even when the stock moves your way. And **spreads** on cheap short-dated options are wide ([Fidelity/OIC](https://fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/learning-center/Deck_Understanding%20options%20pricing.pdf); [Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/option-prices-3-things-investors-need-know)). The research is direct about this. Retail investors favor cheap weekly options with an **average bid-ask spread of 12.6%**, and lost an estimated **$2.1 billion** from November 2019 to June 2021 (evidence: [Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya, Journal of Finance 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/17688.html)). A 12.6% spread means you start about 6% down the moment you buy. For **0DTE** (zero days to expiration, same-day) options, retail traders lost about **$358,000 a day** after daily expirations began in 2022. The losses came mostly from **buying**, especially buying puts, and from transaction costs. Retail selling decisions were actually profitable (evidence: [Beckmeyer, Branger & Gayda via Wealth Management](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/equities/retail-investors-are-in-love-with-same-day-options)). An option buyer has to be right about direction, size of move *and* timing all at once, which is a higher bar than owning the stock.

For your options bot, that points to concrete defaults (rule of thumb, built from the evidence above). Avoid 0DTE and weekly options as a default. Prefer contracts a month or more out on liquid underlying stocks and ETFs with tight spreads and high open interest. Avoid buying right before earnings unless the strategy is explicitly about earnings. Treat the full premium as the amount at risk when sizing.

Prop-firm "challenges" are sometimes held up as proof that trading can be learned. Vendor data across 300,000+ accounts found about a **14% pass rate, and only about 7% of entrants ever paid out**. Firms do not publish audited figures ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/only-1-in-20-traders-pass-prop-firm-challenges-reports-the-funded-trader/); [Track360](https://track360.io/blog/prop-trading-industry-statistics-2026)).

### Risk management is the part you can actually control

**Position sizing** is the main lever for survival. The standard practitioner rule is to risk a small fixed fraction of your account on each trade, commonly **0.5–2%** (rule of thumb). The formula is:

> shares = (account equity × risk %) ÷ (entry price − stop price)

With $20,000 and 1% risk, you can lose $200. With a $2 stop distance, that is 100 shares. For a bought option that might expire worthless, the "stop" is the whole premium, so 1% risk means at most $200 of premium. The math of streaks shows why small fractions matter. Ten straight losses at 1% risk cost about 9.6% of the account. At 10% risk they cost about 65%. And a 50% drawdown needs a 100% gain just to get back to even.

The **Kelly criterion** is a formula for the bet size that maximizes long-run growth: f* = W − (1 − W) ÷ R, where W is the win rate and R is average win divided by average loss. A system with a 50% win rate and 1.5:1 payoff gives f* ≈ 16.7%. Edge estimates are noisy, and betting above the true optimum is punished far more harshly than betting below it, so practitioners use half or quarter Kelly at most (rule of thumb: [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/kelly-criterion/); [NexusFi](https://nexusfi.com/a/risk-management/kelly-criterion)). Treat Kelly as a ceiling, never a target.

Other risk rules complete the picture (rule of thumb). A **daily loss limit** of about 2–3% of the account (or 2–3 times your per-trade risk) stops the bot or you for the day. It guards against **revenge trading**, raising size after a loss to "win it back." Watch **correlation**: five call options on five chip stocks behave like one big bet, so add up risk across related positions. Remember that **stops don't stop gaps**: an earnings-night drop can blow through any stop. And avoid **leverage** you don't need. Margin can force-sell you, and options already contain leverage.

### Expectancy: why win rate alone misleads

**Expectancy** is the average profit per trade:

> expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss)

A system that wins 80% of the time, making $50 per win and losing $300 per loss, loses $20 per trade on average (0.8 × 50 − 0.2 × 300). A system that wins only 35% of the time but makes three times what it risks earns +0.40 of its risk per trade. The break-even win rate is 1 ÷ (1 + reward-to-risk). That is 50% at 1:1 and 33% at 2:1, before costs, and costs push it higher. Retail 0DTE traders were reported to profit on about 49.6% of trades yet lost money in aggregate ([CXO Advisory](https://cxoadvisory.com/individual-investing/retail-0dte-option-trader-performance)). Measure a bot by expectancy after costs, never by win rate.

### Testing: backtests flatter, paper trading flatters, and small samples lie

A **backtest** runs your rules on historical data. Backtests are systematically optimistic. **Look-ahead bias** means using data that wasn't available at decision time. **Survivorship bias** means testing only stocks that still exist today and skipping the ones that went bankrupt. **Overfitting** (or data snooping) means trying many variants and keeping the best, which almost guarantees a flattering result. The Probability of Backtest Overfitting framework shows how easily the "best" configuration turns out worse than average on new data, and it only works if you record every variant you tried (evidence: [Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)). The **Deflated Sharpe Ratio** corrects headline results for the same multiple-testing problem ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/measuring-investment-strategy-snooping-bias/)). The practical fix is **walk-forward testing**. You tune the rules on one stretch of history, test them on the next unseen stretch, repeat, and keep a final period completely untouched until the end.

**Paper trading** (simulated trading with fake money) tests that the bot's mechanics work. It does not test real fills. Alpaca's paper environment fills against real-time quotes but does not model market impact, delay or queue position ([Alpaca Docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading)). Forum users call its fills "notoriously generous" ([Alpaca Forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/slippage-paper-trading-vs-real-trading/2801)). Paper trading also tells you nothing about how you will feel with real money. Assume live results will be worse than paper.

**Sample size** is where most beginners fool themselves. The uncertainty in a win rate is about √(p(1 − p) ÷ n). With a 50% win rate over 100 trades, the true rate could plausibly be anywhere from about 40% to 60%. Over 400 trades it narrows to about ±5 points. For a small edge, say an average of 0.1 of your risk per trade with typical variability, you need **roughly 575 trades** before the result is statistically distinguishable from luck. The 30–50 trades often quoted is far too few, and every extra variant you try raises the bar.

### Psychology, and why a bot helps

The best-documented behavioral finding is that the people who trade most earn least, driven by **overconfidence** (evidence: Barber & Odean, above). Two other well-known biases are the **disposition effect**, selling winners too early and holding losers too long ([Odean](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/disposition/disposition.html)), and **loss aversion**, feeling a loss more sharply than a gain of the same size. A rules-based bot is a real advantage here, because it executes the stop and the size every time. But the bot inherits your biases if you keep overriding it, loosening its stops, or retuning it after every losing week. Keep a **trading journal**, logged automatically by the bot: setup, entry, stop, target, size, risk, outcome, costs and whether the rules were followed. Add a note whenever you intervene manually. That log is what lets you calculate real expectancy (rule of thumb; no study tests whether journaling improves results).

## A three-fund core with automatic rebalancing beats cleverness

### Fix the foundations first

Before investing, pay off high-interest debt. The SEC calls paying down credit cards one of the best "investments" you can make ([Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/news-alerts/investor-bulletins/12-saving-investing-tips-2012)). The average card APR was about **21% in early 2026** ([Motley Fool citing the Federal Reserve](https://www.fool.com/money/research/average-credit-card-interest-rate/)), roughly double the stock market's long-run return, and guaranteed. A sensible order (rule of thumb) is: a small starter emergency fund; any 401(k) employer match; pay off debt above roughly 7–8% interest; build a 3–6 month emergency fund in cash; then invest steadily. The emergency fund is not part of your portfolio. It exists so you never have to sell stocks in a crash.

### Allocation: how much in stocks, bonds and abroad

**Asset allocation**, your mix of stocks, bonds and cash, matters more than which funds you choose. The best-studied default is a **target-date fund**, an all-in-one fund that gets more conservative as its target retirement year approaches. Vanguard's starts at **90% stocks / 10% bonds** and glides to 30% stocks seven years after retirement (evidence: [Vanguard](https://workplace.vanguard.com/investment/strategies/tdf-glide-path.html)). Target-date investors also had essentially **no "behavior gap"** over the decade to 2024, meaning they actually earned their fund's return, unlike most investors (evidence: [Morningstar Mind the Gap 2025](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025)). Age formulas like "110 minus your age in stocks" are untested conveniences (rule of thumb). Time horizon matters more than age: money you need within about five years should not be mostly in stocks.

Bonds earn their place by cushioning crashes. In the 17 down years for the S&P 500 since 1945, stocks averaged −13% while 5-year Treasuries averaged **+5.3%** (evidence: [A Wealth of Common Sense](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2025/05/60-40-portfolio-corrections-bear-markets-and-recoveries/)). 2022 was the exception, when both fell together as rates spiked ([Dimensional](https://dimensional.com/us-en/insights/the-60-40-portfolio-down-but-not-out)). For international stocks, Vanguard found volatility fell most with 40–50% of stocks abroad, and treats the global market weight (non-US roughly 37% in its study) as a starting point, not a target ([ETF.com summarizing Vanguard](https://www.etf.com/sections/index-investor-corner/swedroe-benefits-intl-diversification); [Funds Society](https://www.fundssociety.com/en/news/etf/are-investors-too-concentrated-in-u-s-mega-caps/)). A reasonable range is **20–40% of your stocks outside the US**.

The simplest do-it-yourself version is the **three-fund portfolio**: a US total stock market fund, an international total stock fund and a US total bond fund. Typical ETFs are VTI/VXUS/BND or ITOT/IXUS/AGG (rule of thumb, Bogleheads convention). A young investor's version might be 54% US stocks, 36% international and 10% bonds. That is a 90/10 mix with 40% of stocks abroad, which lines up with the Vanguard evidence. For a long-term bot, this is an ideal job: hold these three funds, add new money to whichever is furthest below target, and do little else.

### Core and satellite: where your trading bots fit

The **core-satellite** approach puts most of the portfolio, commonly **80–95%, in a low-cost index core**, and a small satellite, commonly **5–10%**, into individual stocks or active trading (rule of thumb). Bessembinder's skew and SPIVA's scoreboard justify the shape: the satellite is money you can afford to underperform with. Track it separately so you know whether it adds anything. A diversified portfolio of about 30–40 random stocks removes most day-to-day volatility (evidence: [Statman, JFQA 1987](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/how-many-stocks-make-a-diversified-portfolio/CE5CDF2C7225FC1E0EDE3E700A3C66A7)). But it can still miss the rare giant winners, which is why the core should be the whole market. Common position caps (rule of thumb) are no more than **5% of the portfolio in any single stock** and 20–25% in any one sector.

### Rebalance yearly, invest lump sums promptly, and don't flinch

**Rebalancing** means trading back to your target mix after markets move it. It controls risk; it is not a way to boost returns. Vanguard's research supports checking once or twice a year and acting when an asset class has **drifted about 5 percentage points** from target (evidence: [Vanguard Canada](https://www.vanguard.ca/en/investor/insights/when_and_how_to_rebalance); [Vanguard, Rational rebalancing](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/rational_rebalancing_analytical_approach_to_multiasset_portfolio_rebalancing.pdf)). Threshold rules trade less than calendar rules and are expected to return slightly more ([Vanguard, The rebalancing edge](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/the_rebalancing_edge_optimizing_target_date_fund_rebalancing_through_threshold_based_strategies.pdf)). In a taxable account, rebalance by directing new money to the underweight fund rather than selling. A long-term bot can use exactly this rule: check on a schedule, act only past the threshold.

If you get a lump sum, investing it all at once beat spreading it out (**dollar-cost averaging**, or DCA) about **68% of the time** in Vanguard's 1976–2022 data, because markets rise more often than they fall. DCA still beat sitting in cash about 69% of the time (evidence: [Vanguard](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/cost_averaging_invest_now_or_temporarily_hold_your_cash.pdf)). If fear of a crash would otherwise keep you in cash, spreading the money over 3–6 months is a reasonable compromise. Investing from each paycheck is simply investing money as you earn it, which is good.

Your own behavior is the biggest cost you control. Investors earned about **1.2 percentage points a year less than the funds they owned** over the decade to 2024, because they bought and sold at the wrong times. The gap was bigger for sector funds and high-turnover funds, and near zero for target-date funds (evidence: [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025); [Boldin summary](https://www.boldin.com/retirement/why-investors-miss-out-on-fund-returns/)). Missing just the **10 best days over two decades roughly halved ending wealth**, and seven of those best days came within two weeks of the worst days (evidence: [J.P. Morgan via 401k Specialist](https://401kspecialistmag.com/folly-of-timing-the-market-explained-in-jpmams-2024-guide-to-retirement/)). Among about 298,000 brokerage households, panic sellers often waited too long to get back in, and the median panic seller earned zero or negative returns afterwards. Investors who rated their own knowledge as "excellent" panicked more often (evidence: [Elkind, Kaminski, Lo et al., MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/141712)). Your bots should never be allowed to "time" the core portfolio by selling it on a signal.

## Dividends are part of total return, not free money

A **dividend** is cash a company pays to shareholders out of its profits. Four dates matter. The **declaration date** is when the board announces it. The **ex-dividend date** is the cutoff: you must own the shares before it. The **record date** is when the company checks its list of holders. The **payment date** is when the cash arrives. Since the move to T+1 settlement in May 2024, the **ex-date and the record date are the same day** for regular cash dividends, so buy at least one business day before the ex-date (evidence: [SEC, NYSE rule filing](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nysenat/2024/34-99873.pdf); [The Corporate Counsel](https://www.thecorporatecounsel.net/blog/2024/04/nyse-rule-changes-to-implement-t1.html)). On the ex-date the share price drops by roughly the dividend amount. A $100 stock paying $1 opens around $99, and you receive $1 in cash. Your wealth is unchanged, except that you now owe tax on the $1.

Key measures (standard definitions). **Dividend yield** is annual dividends ÷ price. **Payout ratio** is dividends ÷ earnings. The stricter **free-cash-flow payout** is dividends ÷ free cash flow. The **dividend growth rate** is how fast payments have grown. A **DRIP** (dividend reinvestment plan) automatically buys more shares with each dividend. Reinvested dividends are still taxable that year, and each purchase becomes a new tax lot ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/how-to-calculate/cost-basis-in-dividend-reinvestment-plans)). The whole US market yields **below 1.2%**, extremely low by historical standards ([Morningstar](https://global.morningstar.com/en-gb/stocks/how-income-investors-can-avoid-dividend-traps-2026)).

**Taxes.** **Qualified dividends** are taxed at the long-term capital gains rates of 0/15/20% shown earlier. To qualify, you must hold common stock **more than 60 days during the 121-day window that starts 60 days before the ex-date** ([IRS Pub 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550); [White Coat Investor](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/the-60-day-qualified-dividend-rule/)). Everything else is taxed as ordinary income. That includes most **REIT** (real estate investment trust) dividends, which get a 20% deduction made permanent in 2025 ([Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/client-alerts/reit-all-about-it-one-big-beautiful-bill-tax-updates-for-reits)). It also includes the income from covered-call funds such as JEPI, where an ~8.4% headline yield nets ~5.7% for someone in the 32% bracket ([ETF.com](https://www.etf.com/sections/news/what-covered-call-etf)). The general rule (rule of thumb) is to hold high-yield and non-qualified payers inside IRAs, and qualified payers in taxable accounts.

**Popular dividend funds** are really style tilts. SCHD (~0.06% fee, ~3.1–3.3% yield) and VYM (~2.2–2.5% yield) lean toward value stocks. VIG (~1.5–1.7% yield) leans toward quality and dividend growth ([DividendVision](https://www.dividendvision.com/compare/schd-vs-vig-vs-vym)). These are third-party snapshots; check the issuer pages. **Dividend Aristocrats** are S&P 500 members with 25+ straight years of raises, numbering 67–69 in 2026 depending on the source ([Simply Safe Dividends](https://simplysafedividends.com/world-of-dividends/posts/6-2026-dividend-aristocrats-list-all-69-ranked-analyzed)). **Covered-call ETFs** trade away upside for income. In 2023 the S&P 500 returned about 26% while JEPI returned just under 10% ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/funds/should-you-own-covered-call-etf-like-jepi)).

**The evidence.** In theory, dividends don't create wealth: Miller and Modigliani showed that with no taxes or frictions, an investor wanting cash could simply sell shares (a "homemade dividend") ([Lexplug summary](https://www.lexplug.com/outlines/corporate-finance/dividend-irrelevance-mm)). In practice, investors treat dividends as free extra income. Hartzmark and Solomon call this the **"free dividends fallacy"**. They estimate that buying dividend stocks when demand for them is high costs **2–4% a year** in expected returns (evidence: [Journal of Finance 2019](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:74:y:2019:i:5:p:2153-2199); [CFA Institute Digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2020/05/dig-v5-n5-4)). Once value and profitability are accounted for, high yield adds essentially nothing. One study found a high-dividend group's alpha fell from 2.4% to an insignificant −0.6% under a fuller factor model (evidence: [FA Mag](https://www.fa-mag.com/news/challenging-the-high-dividend-yield-stock-narrative-77839.html); [FPA Journal](https://www.financialplanningassociation.org/article/journal/APR13-dividend-investing-value-tilt-disguise)). The famous claim that dividends supplied **about 40% of the S&P 500's total return** since 1930 ([Hartford Funds](https://HARTFORDFUNDS.COM/dam/en/docs/pub/whitepapers/WP106.pdf)) shows that *reinvesting* matters, which an index fund already does automatically. It does not show that dividend stocks beat the market.

**Yield traps** are stocks whose high yield signals trouble. Morningstar's 20-year research found **high payout ratios were the strongest predictor of dividend cuts**. Dow paid out 341.5% of earnings in 2023 and Walgreens 290.9% before its cut. Morningstar's dividend indexes exclude payout ratios above 100% (evidence: [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/not-all-dividend-stocks-are-safe-heres-how-avoid-dividend-traps)). **Dividend capture**, buying just before the ex-date and selling just after, does not work for US retail investors. The price drop offsets the dividend, the short hold makes the dividend non-qualified, and spreads finish the job (opinion/secondary: [DividendRanks](https://dividendranks.com/learn/strategy/dividend-capture-strategy); [ilmatematico review](https://ilmatematico.substack.com/p/dividend-capture-strategy-analysis)). Don't build a bot around it.

If you want to live on dividends, the math is unforgiving. The capital you need is your annual income ÷ yield. **$40,000 a year needs about $3.3 million at the market's 1.2% yield, $1.25 million at SCHD's ~3.2%**, or $1 million under a 4% total-return withdrawal rule. Vanguard's research favors the total-return approach: spend from dividends *and* sold gains under a sound withdrawal rule, rather than chasing yield, which concentrates the portfolio and raises taxes ([Vanguard total-return investing](https://www.firstlinks.com.au/uploads/2022/documents/Vanguard-total-return-investing.pdf)). Dividends are fine; just judge every holding on **total return** (price change plus dividends), not yield.

## Judging a bot: beat the SPY shadow account after costs, taxes and luck

Every bot needs a benchmark, and for a US stock bot the honest one is **SPY held and never touched**. Since professionals trail it most of the time, the burden of proof sits with the bot. Run a "shadow" account alongside each bot: same start date, same deposits, all in SPY. Then compare after commissions, spreads, slippage and taxes. Remember that the bot's gains are all short-term and SPY's would be long-term. The standard measures below are textbook definitions.

| Measure | What it means | How to use it on a bot |
|---|---|---|
| CAGR | Compound annual growth rate | Headline return, after all costs |
| Volatility | Annualized standard deviation of returns (daily × √252) | How bumpy the ride is; S&P 500 is commonly ~15–20% |
| Max drawdown | Largest peak-to-trough fall | Compare with SPY's over the same dates |
| Sharpe ratio | (Return − T-bill rate) ÷ volatility | Return per unit of risk; must beat SPY's Sharpe |
| Beta | Sensitivity to the market (1 = moves with S&P 500) | A beta-0.5 bot should be compared with 50% SPY / 50% cash |
| Alpha | Return beyond what beta explains | The only part that reflects skill |
| Expectancy | Average profit per trade, in dollars or in units of risk | Must be positive after costs |

A bot that returns less than SPY with much smaller drawdowns can still be worthwhile if its Sharpe ratio is higher. A bot with higher returns but a lower Sharpe is just taking more risk. Two further checks (rule of thumb). First, see whether the profits come from a handful of trades; if they do, it is probably luck. Second, wait for a long enough sample: a few hundred trades, or a full market cycle for slower strategies. High-turnover funds trail their own holdings by 2–3% a year ([Boldin, summarizing Morningstar](https://www.boldin.com/retirement/why-investors-miss-out-on-fund-returns/)), and a fast-trading bot faces the same headwind.

Crypto works differently, as covered in the earlier report *Crypto day trading on Alpaca*. On Alpaca, crypto trades 24/7, long-only, with no shorting or margin. Fees at the starting tier are 0.15% for resting limit orders and 0.25% for market orders, so a market-order round trip costs **about 0.5% before the spread** ([Alpaca Docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/crypto-fees)). The crypto bot's benchmark is buy-and-hold Bitcoin, its trading should focus on BTC and ETH during weekday daytime hours, and meme coins should be a capped sleeve of no more than about 10% of the crypto money.

## Conclusion

The research turns the usual beginner story upside down. The parts of investing that feel like skill (picking stocks, reading patterns, timing trades) have the weakest evidence. The parts that feel dull (low costs, broad diversification, the right account, holding past one year, small position sizes and not panicking) have the strongest. The 2026 rule changes widen this gap rather than closing it. Dropping the $25,000 day-trading minimum removed a guardrail without improving anyone's odds, so the limit on trade count now has to live in your bot's code. Cheap, nearly 24-hour access makes it easier to trade more, and trading more is the habit the evidence punishes most consistently.

That reframes what your Claude-assisted bots are for. Their best use is not finding an edge the professionals missed. It is enforcing discipline you set in advance: rebalancing the core on a schedule, sizing every trade by formula, placing every stop, stopping for the day after a set loss, and logging everything honestly. Treat the active bots as an experiment with a pre-registered success test and a capped budget. If after several hundred trades they cannot beat the SPY or Bitcoin shadow account after costs and taxes, the evidence-based move is to shut them down and let the core portfolio do the work. That is a successful experiment, not a failure: you will have learned it with a small slice of your money.

## Action plan and checklist

### Step 1: Foundations (before any investing)

1. Pay off credit cards and any debt above roughly 7–8% interest.
2. Keep a 3–6 month emergency fund in cash or a high-yield savings account, outside the brokerage.
3. If you have a 401(k) with an employer match, contribute at least enough to get the full match.
4. Open and fund a Roth IRA if you are eligible (2026 limit $7,500), or a Traditional IRA otherwise. Consider whether your broker lets you hold the long-term bot's core inside an IRA, where trades are not taxed each year.

### Step 2: Account setup on Alpaca

1. Start in **paper trading** for every bot. Keep live and paper API keys separate and clearly labeled in the app.
2. Choose a **cash account** for the live trading bots unless you have a specific reason for margin. If you use a cash account, have the bot track settled cash to avoid good-faith and freeriding violations.
3. Confirm how Alpaca applies the new **intraday margin standard** (it may still use PDT-style limits during the phase-in to October 2027), what options approval level you have, and whether live crypto is enabled in your state.
4. Give API keys **trading-only** permissions, with no withdrawals or transfers.
5. Turn on automatic **trade logging**: every order, fill price, the bid/ask at the time, fees, the reason for the trade, and any manual override.

### Step 3: Build the core portfolio (80–95% of investable money)

1. Pick either a single **target-date fund** or a **three-fund portfolio** (US total market, international total market, US total bond).
2. Set a target mix. Example for someone decades from retirement: 90% stocks / 10% bonds, with 30–40% of stocks international. Shift toward bonds for money needed within about 5 years.
3. Invest lump sums promptly, or over 3–6 months if fear would otherwise keep you in cash. Automate regular contributions.
4. Let the **long-term bot** do only this: buy with new money into the most underweight fund, check once or twice a year, and rebalance only when a fund drifts more than about 5 percentage points from target. In taxable accounts, rebalance with new money first.
5. Hold for more than a year to get long-term tax rates. The core is never sold on a trading signal.

### Step 4: Size the active "satellite" (5–10% of investable money, maximum)

1. Cap **all** active strategies combined (stock picks, day-trading bot, options bot, crypto bot) at **5–10% of investable assets**. Start at the low end, and only with money you could lose without changing your life.
2. A suggested split of that sleeve (rule of thumb): about half for swing or day trading of stocks and ETFs, no more than a quarter for options buying, and no more than a quarter for crypto, with meme coins at most 10% of the crypto money.
3. Single individual stocks: no more than 5% of the total portfolio each; no more than 20–25% in one sector.
4. Never add money to the satellite to "make back" losses. Refill it only on a set schedule (for example, once a year), if at all.

### Step 5: Hard-code the risk rules into every bot

1. **Risk per trade:** 0.5–1% of that bot's account (the distance from entry to stop × shares). For bought options, the full premium counts as the risk, so premium per trade is at most 1–2% of the account.
2. **Daily loss limit:** stop opening trades after a loss of about 2–3% of the account in a day. **Weekly limit:** about 5%, then pause for review.
3. **Trade-count cap:** set a maximum number of round trips per day (for example, 3–6), now that the PDT rule no longer limits you.
4. **Correlation cap:** limit the total risk in related positions (same sector, same index) to roughly one or two trades' worth.
5. **Orders:** use limit orders by default; no market orders in the first 15–30 minutes, in extended hours, or in the planned overnight session. Every entry is placed with its stop and target (bracket orders for stocks; code-managed exits for crypto).
6. **Options defaults:** no 0DTE or weekly contracts by default; prefer a month or more to expiration; trade only liquid contracts with tight spreads and high open interest; skip buying right before earnings unless that is the tested strategy.
7. **Gaps:** decide in advance whether swing positions may be held through earnings. Assume any stop can be jumped overnight.
8. **No leverage** beyond what an option itself contains. No margin borrowing for beginners.
9. **Kill switch:** pause the bot on stale data, unusually wide spreads, repeated order rejections, or any move larger than about 3 × ATR within minutes.

### Step 6: Test a bot before it touches real money

1. Write the rules down completely before testing: universe, entry, stop, target, exit timing, size.
2. Backtest with realistic costs (spreads, slippage, fees), include delisted stocks if possible, and record **every** variant you try.
3. Use walk-forward testing and keep a final untouched period for one last check.
4. Paper trade the exact rules with no mid-test tweaks. Subtract an extra slippage haircut from paper results, because paper fills are generous.
5. Go live at 10–25% of the planned size only after the paper results pass Step 7. Compare live fills with paper fills for the same signals.

### Step 7: Judge whether a bot is working

1. Run a **shadow benchmark** from the same start date with the same deposits: SPY for stock and options bots, buy-and-hold BTC for the crypto bot.
2. Each month, compare net-of-cost and estimated after-tax CAGR, volatility, max drawdown, Sharpe ratio, beta and alpha against the shadow account.
3. Measure **expectancy** after costs (average result per trade, in units of risk), not win rate.
4. Don't draw conclusions before **a few hundred trades**. For a small edge, about 575 trades are needed to tell skill from luck. Weeks of good results prove nothing.
5. Check that profits are not concentrated in a handful of trades, and that the bot followed its rules (count manual overrides).
6. **Decision rules (set them now):** keep or scale up only if the bot beats the shadow account on a risk-adjusted, after-cost basis over the full sample. Cut size in half after a drawdown twice as deep as the backtest's worst. Shut the bot down if it hits the drawdown limit you set before launch, or if after the full sample it still trails the benchmark.

### Step 8: Taxes and records

1. Know the one-year line: bot profits are short-term (taxed at ordinary rates). The core should be held for more than a year.
2. Watch for **wash sales** when a bot re-buys a ticker within 30 days of selling it at a loss, including purchases in your IRA.
3. Harvest losses in the taxable core when sensible. Up to $3,000 a year of net losses offsets ordinary income.
4. Keep the bot's own trade log alongside the broker's 1099-B and 1099-DA, and consider a tax professional once you have hundreds of trades.

### Ongoing habits

1. Don't check the core portfolio daily. Review it once or twice a year.
2. Never let a bot, a headline or a bad week sell the core portfolio.
3. Re-read this checklist before raising any bot's size.
